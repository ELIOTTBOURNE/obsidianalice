# Franklin Roosevelt and Entry into the Second World War, 1937-41

## *Roosevelt's Early Presidency and Isolationism*

Franklin D. Roosevelt was elected president in 1932. At the height of the Great Depression, Roosevelt's priority was to repair the American economy and institute the New Deal programme: during his first administration (1933-37) foreign policy had a low priority.

Roosevelt's own beliefs were firmly in the Wilsonian tradition. However, Roosevelt was a shrewd political operator and, all too aware of the popular isolationist trend in US politics, at first he did little to challenge isolationism or resist the Neutrality Acts passed in 1935-37.

Nevertheless, the international situation was deteriorating and darkening in the mid-1930s:

* A resurgent Germany under its new leader Adolf Hitler was rearming and breaking the terms of the Versailles Treaty.  
* The Italian dictator Benito Mussolini had attacked Ethiopia in defiance of the League of Nations.  
* The Spanish civil war had begun and rapidly involved Germany and Italy on the fascist side.  
* Japan had invaded China, massacring thousands of Chinese civilians in Nanking in 1937.

Roosevelt began to question the wisdom of isolationism.

## *The Strains of Neutrality*

In October 1937, Roosevelt questioned whether neutrality and isolation were adequate responses. Controversially, he suggested that aggressive nations should be *quarantined*. Whether he meant by economic sanctions or by the breaking of diplomatic relations, he did not make clear. There was an isolationist backlash in the press and Congress. Roosevelt quickly denied that he had any specific actions in mind.

In December 1937 Japanese aircraft sank the American gunboat *Panay* in the Yangtze river, in China, killing three US sailors and injuring 45. The reaction of the American public was muted compared to the outrage over the *Maine* and *Lusitania*. In fact the most common public reaction was to call for the removal of American ships from the Far East. The US government sent a protest; the Japanese apologised.

Reined in by the Neutrality Acts and US military unpreparedness, Roosevelt was reduced to a spectator's role during the 1938 Munich crisis. Neville Chamberlain, the British prime minister, contemptuously dismissed Roosevelt's idea of a world conference on disarmament and political problems, and embarked on his own policy of *appeasement*, remarking that 'nothing can be expected from the Americans but words'.

Nevertheless, America's woeful military unpreparedness was clear enough to isolationists as well as Roosevelt. In a significant move in 1938, Congress approved a Naval Expansion Act which was the largest peacetime funding for the navy to that date. In early 1939 Roosevelt also obtained $525 million funding to expand the air force.

## *The Road to War*

### *Neutrality*

The outbreak of a European war in September 1939 prompted the expected proclamation of neutrality, but there was a difference from the 1914 declaration. In one of his *fireside chats* Roosevelt remarked that 'even a neutral cannot be asked to close his mind or his conscience'.

The Neutrality Acts were, in his view, an obstacle and he called Congress into special session to revise the neutrality legislation. After much argument and debate, Congress passed a new Neutrality Act in November 1939:

* The arms embargo was repealed  
* Belligerents could buy arms on a *cash and carry* basis

The ban on American loans to belligerents, however, remained.

### *Changing Attitudes Towards Isolationism*

Attitudes about the wisdom of isolationism were now changing in the USA:

* Adolf Hitler's destruction of the Munich settlement and Germany's attack on Poland in 1939 had shown his aggressive intentions.  
* German victories in 1940 over France and Britain had come as a rude shock — no one had expected France to be defeated in six weeks and for Britain's army to be ejected from the continent.  
* Germany's domination of Europe was now perceived as a threat to American security, particularly if Germany should control the British and French fleets and the whole east Atlantic shore.  
* Britain's continuing resistance to Germany and its success in the Battle of Britain did inspire sympathy and support in the USA. A new prime minister, Winston Churchill, made the most of his American ancestry and consistent support of the USA. Ed Murrow's radio broadcasts from a London under attack in the *Blitz* made a powerful impact on American public opinion.  
* Hitler's well-publicised racist policies and the attacks on Jews on *Kristallnacht* in 1938 had caused fear and revulsion in equal measure in the minds of many Americans.  
* The Tripartite Pact between Germany, Italy and Japan in September 1940 was a mutual aid treaty clearly intended to deter the USA from intervening against them.

### *Protecting US Security*

It was not hard for Roosevelt to argue that support for Britain was essential to protect America's security. He found two ways to circumvent the Neutrality Act: in an executive order in 1940 he arranged:

* The transfer of 'surplus' military equipment to Britain  
* The transfer of 50 destroyers to Britain in exchange for 90-year leases of bases for the USA in six British colonies

The British action in July 1940 in ruthlessly destroying or demobilising the French fleet to prevent it falling into German hands did much to reassure the USA.

Roosevelt secured congressional backing for the continued expansion of American defence:

* Grants to the navy and air force were even larger than those of 1938-39, including a staggering commitment to build 50,000 aircraft a year.  
* More controversial was the unprecedented introduction of peacetime conscription in September 1940 to expand all of the US armed forces.  
* A National Defence Research Committee was established in June 1940 to coordinate work on new weapons (eventually this would develop the atomic bomb).

### *Opposition to Roosevelt*

Roosevelt's proactive foreign policy and especially the decision to exchange the destroyers prompted a fierce reaction.

A group of Chicago businessmen supported the formation of an **America First Committee** strongly supporting isolationism, asserting that Hitler's Germany was not a threat and that support for Britain was an unnecessary risk.

America First did not object to strengthening America's defences but it wanted to keep out of any war. The movement was discredited to some extent by some of the political oddballs who joined its ranks, notably the notorious anti-Semite Father Coughlin (a vocal opponent of Roosevelt), communists and outright Nazi supporters like the German-American Bund.

Foreign policy was naturally a key issue in the 1940 presidential election and Roosevelt exhibited all his customary caution, promising that 'American boys would not be sent into any foreign war'. He still won but less convincingly than in 1932 and 1936 — America's first and only third-term president.

## *How Did Roosevelt's Actions 1940-41 Change the Direction of US Foreign Policy?*

### *Lend-Lease*

Convinced that the USA needed to support Britain's continuing resistance, Roosevelt knew that loans were out of the question because of the Neutrality Act restrictions. Britain's plight could not wait upon a prolonged debate in Congress to alter the Acts further. Instead the president devised an ingenious solution to his problem — instead of lending money, the USA would lend goods.

The **Lend-Lease Bill** that Roosevelt sent to Congress would enable the USA to sell, lease or lend arms, munitions and other war supplies to any country whose defence was vital to the security of the USA.

In December 1940 he compared his scheme to the idea of lending a garden hose to a neighbour whose house was on fire. In one of his most successful fireside chats a few days later, he explained that if Britain collapsed, America would be living 'at the point of a gun' and that his main purpose was to keep war away from America by bolstering Britain. In a phrase that would resonate, America would become '*the great arsenal of democracy*'. Opinion polls registered 80% approval for the president's speech. Congress was less convinced and two months of wrangling ensued, but the bill was passed and Britain received a staggering $7 billion of aid in the first Lend-Lease appropriation. In Germany the propaganda minister, Dr Goebbels, described Lend-Lease as a declaration of war.

### *Confronting Germany*

The benefits of Lend-Lease could only work if the Atlantic Ocean could be kept open: here the German U-boats were sinking 500,000 tons of merchant shipping a month in the spring of 1941. If Britain was to survive, the Atlantic convoys had to defeat the U-boat threat.

Roosevelt had probably decided by the summer of 1941 that the USA would be involved in the war. However, in order to keep the country united the USA would have to be attacked before it could contemplate a declaration of war. That attack was likely to be in the Atlantic Ocean. Roosevelt's actions, cautiously incremental, were nevertheless extraordinary for a country that was technically neutral and reveal the depth of his commitment to Britain:

* In March 1941, permission was granted to repair British warships in American shipyards.  
* In April, he extended a neutrality zone halfway across the Atlantic, ordering the US navy to patrol and report the position of German U-boats to British warships.  
* In July, American troops occupied Iceland, a key strategic base in the Atlantic. The navy was instructed to organise convoys of American vessels.  
* In August, Roosevelt met Churchill in a blaze of publicity off the coast of Newfoundland. They agreed and announced the **Atlantic Charter**, which promised:  
  * self-determination  
  * equal access to trade and raw materials  
  * freedom of the seas  
  * disarmament  
  * freedom from fear and want Short of calling them war aims, it was the nearest thing to an informal alliance.  
* In September, Roosevelt exploited an incident where the US destroyer *Greer* was attacked by a German U-boat. Concealing the fact that the *Greer* had been shadowing the U-boat and reporting its presence to the British, he seized the opportunity to present it as an act of piracy and announced that U-boats in American-patrolled waters would be sunk on sight. In a fireside chat, German submarines were described as rattlesnakes: 'when you see a rattlesnake poised to strike you do not wait until he has struck before you crush him'.  
* In October, the USS *Kearney* and the USS *Reuben Jones* were attacked by U-boats with great loss of life. A week later Congress repealed all the Neutrality Acts by slim majorities. In effect, by the autumn of 1941 America was fighting an undeclared war against Germany in the Atlantic.

In Berlin, Hitler had come to the same conclusion. Only his preoccupation with the attack on Russia in the summer and autumn of 1941 prevented him from retaliating against Roosevelt's actions.

## *War with Japan*

Japan's continuing aggression against China became even more menacing in 1940 with Germany's defeat and occupation of France and the Netherlands. The signing of the Tripartite Pact in September 1940 added to American fears.

Roosevelt's administration imposed economic sanctions to deter the Japanese. These sanctions had no impact upon Japanese policy. Indeed, the weak *Vichy government* was forced to allow the Japanese to set up bases in French Indo-China. Roosevelt increased the pressure on the Japanese by:

* Freezing Japanese assets in the USA in July 1941  
* Closing the Panama Canal to Japanese shipping  
* Banning all oil exports to Japan from August 1941

Negotiations between the Japanese and Americans started, but in September 1941 Roosevelt demanded Japanese withdrawal from China and the Tripartite Pact. The protection of China was clearly his priority. He was concerned about US trade with China and the vulnerability of American territory in the Philippines.

His tough approach encouraged the *Japanese militarists* to plan for war, aiming to seize vital raw materials in the Far East by knocking out the American fleet at Pearl Harbor in Hawaii.

It used to be believed that Roosevelt deliberately antagonised the Japanese to provoke war. This view is no longer taken seriously:

* Roosevelt and his advisers did not want a war in the Pacific; they wanted to protect China and deter Japanese aggression.  
* Although they knew that a Japanese attack was planned, they did not know where. The consensus view was that it was to be an attack on southeast Asia against British and Dutch colonies to secure oil and rubber.  
* The naval base at Pearl Harbor was alerted, but the message did not get through in time.

In the event the Japanese confounded the Americans by attacking everywhere. A carrier-based force attacked and crippled the US Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941. Simultaneously, Japanese invasion convoys attacked British and Dutch possessions in Malaya and Borneo, and American possessions in the Philippines and Wake Island.

The attack on Pearl Harbor aroused universal condemnation in America; no more was heard from America First and Congress declared war on Japan on 8 December 1941. Three days later Hitler declared war on the USA, fulfilling his role in the Tripartite Pact.

## *The USA and the Second World War*

### *The Economic Superpower*

The war brought about a huge expansion of America's productive capacity to meet not only the needs of its own armed forces but also massive Lend-Lease shipments to its allies:

* America's manufacturing output, already large, doubled between 1941 and 1945  
* America produced 60% of the world's oil and 50% of its steel.  
* It was, however, in the production of military equipment that the USA reached staggering totals: for example it produced 300,000 aircraft during the war.  
* American shipyards mass produced the Liberty ship, a simple steel-framed merchant ship, which enabled the Allies to sustain their losses from U-boats and to supply vital convoys, while also creating the capacity to mount the largest amphibious operations in military history.  
* By 1945 the USA had the largest navy in the world, dwarfing that of Britain's, the next largest.  
* Some 15 million Americans were in military service by 1945.

The sheer scale of American production of weapons and military equipment was a major factor in the Allied victory.

By 1945 the economies of the European countries were either financially bankrupt or wrecked by the effects of war: the USA was now the only economic superpower and it was in a position of superiority even greater than that of 1918-19.

### *Fighting the War*

There were a number of important differences between the US experience in the Second World War and that in the First:

* Unlike in 1917-18 the Americans had to undertake massive military, naval and air operations in two widely separated areas, in the Pacific against Japan and in Europe against Germany and Italy.  
* In the First World War the USA had fought as an associated power. In the Second World War, it merged its efforts with its allies, collaborating particularly closely with Britain. On 1 January 1942, Britain, the USA and USSR plus 23 other nations at war with the *Axis* powers signed a Declaration of the United Nations:  
  * upholding the principles of the Atlantic Charter  
  * deploying all their resources against their enemies  
  * undertaking not to sign a separate peace It was the first binding military agreement the USA had signed since 1778.  
* Britain and the USA coordinated their military strategy from the start. A combined Chiefs of Staff committee planned cooperation. Roosevelt and Churchill worked well together despite occasional tensions, most particularly over Roosevelt's dislike of the British Empire. Their forces fought together in the invasions of north Africa and Sicily in 1942-43 and of northwest Europe in June 1944 (D-Day) — there was an overall American commander-in-chief, while the land, naval and air forces involved were each commanded by British officers. The British Pacific Fleet served under American command in the final stages of the war against Japan.  
* There were some disagreements over strategy — in 1942-43 the British view prevailed, while in 1944-45 the American view prevailed as its military contribution far exceeded that of Britain.  
* Relations between the western allies and the USSR were more problematical. Although the alliance held together and Germany was crushed by 1945, wartime conferences between Roosevelt and Stalin at Teheran in 1943 and Yalta in 1945 revealed growing tensions:  
  * The Russians resented Allied delays in launching an invasion of Europe while they were fighting most of the German army.  
  * Stalin was by nature paranoid and suspicious. He had forgotten neither British and American intervention in 1918, in the civil war which followed the Russian Revolution, nor Churchill's long history of anti-communism.  
  * Stalin's priority was Russian security after the war. This conflicted with the western allies' demands for free elections in eastern European countries, which might elect anti-communist governments.  
  * The western allies resented what they saw as Russian ingratitude for the huge amounts of aid given to the Russian war effort by Britain and America.  
* Roosevelt was determined that post-war security should be based on the United Nations. This was envisaged not just as a framework for resolving disputes, but significantly as one for resolving economic problems and ensuring adequate food supplies and famine relief.  
* In contrast to Wilson, Roosevelt obtained overwhelming approval for the United Nations Charter from Congress in 1943. The organisation was eventually set up in San Francisco in 1945 and, significantly, its headquarters were to be in New York. America's new commitment to internationalism was clear.  
* Only the USA had the resources and drive to construct a viable atomic weapon — the Manhattan project. In great secrecy $2 billion of federal expenditure resourced a team of British, American and Canadian scientists and nearly 90,000 workers. In July 1945 a test weapon was successfully detonated in New Mexico. One bomb had the capacity to destroy an entire city. The nuclear age had begun, with enormous implications for international relations.

## *America's Military Role in Allied Success*

### *War Against Japan*

America played the major role in the defeat of Japan. Although the American fleet had been badly damaged in the attack on Pearl Harbor, the Japanese had missed the oil installations and the aircraft carriers. By 1942 the aircraft carrier had emerged as the most powerful naval vessel.

In two decisive battles in May and June 1942, at the Coral Sea and Midway, the Americans destroyed four of Japan's six fleet aircraft carriers and badly damaged a fifth. The Japanese navy's capacity for offensive operations never recovered.

The Pacific war consisted of complex amphibious operations to retake strategic islands, starting with Guadalcanal in 1942-43 and culminating in the capture of the Philippines and the islands of Okinawa and Iwo Jima by 1945. American bombers were now within range of Japan. The Americans encountered fanatical resistance from the Japanese garrisons, resulting in very heavy casualties on both sides. At the Battle of Leyte Gulf in October 1944, the Japanese navy was finally overwhelmed.

Most of the Japanese army was in fact deployed against China. America kept the Chinese well supplied throughout the war so that the Japanese could be tied down in China. The important supply route to China was the Burma Road and Britain played a significant part in keeping this route open by completely destroying the Japanese army in Burma in 1944-45.

By 1945 the Americans and British were preparing to invade Japan itself. As agreed at the Yalta conference, the Soviet Union declared war on Japan in August 1945 and invaded Manchuria. President Truman (Roosevelt had died in April) took the decision to use the new atomic bomb to bring the war to an end. Two Japanese cities, Hiroshima and Nagasaki, were destroyed with heavy loss of life (possibly 80,000 deaths at Hiroshima and 35,000 at Nagasaki) in two separate atomic attacks on 6 August and 9 August 1945. Five days later Japan surrendered.

The decision to use atomic weapons has excited controversy ever since:

* It has been claimed that it was unnecessary as the Japanese would have surrendered anyway and that the main reason for their use was to intimidate the Soviet Union.  
* It is more likely that Truman's decision was based on the need to end the war quickly with fewer American casualties.  
* As it was, the Japanese did not surrender immediately — some army leaders wanted to fight on after the atomic bombs had been used.  
* At the time there were few doubts about the use of the weapons.

### *War in Europe*

In Europe the Americans (and the Russians) were keen that the Allies should invade northwest Europe in overwhelming force as soon as possible. The British argued that this needed careful preparation and planning, and that the Allies did not have sufficient strength in 1942-43.

Britain was already fighting the Germans and Italians in the Mediterranean and argued strongly that this should be the Allies' first priority. A bad British defeat at Tobruk in north Africa in June 1942 had already persuaded Roosevelt to give the British 300 of the USA's latest tank — the Sherman — and convoy them directly to Egypt. A revitalised British army defeated the Germans and Italians at El Alamein in October-November 1942. Roosevelt agreed (overruling his military advisers) to launch Operation Torch, an Allied invasion of north Africa to complete the Axis defeat. By May 1943 the combined British and American forces had won an important victory in north Africa with the capture of 250,000 Axis soldiers, freeing up the Mediterranean.

The British argued strongly for the continuation of Allied offensives in the Mediterranean by attacking Italy, believing that this would indirectly weaken Germany. The Americans were less convinced but went along with the British plans by invading Sicily in a huge amphibious operation in July 1943, followed by the invasion of Italy and its surrender in September 1943. The Allied conference at Teheran in November-December 1943 confirmed, however, that the long-awaited second front in northwest Europe would no longer be delayed.

Commanded by US General Eisenhower, the invasion of northwest Europe was launched on D-Day, 6 June 1944, in a combined British, American and Canadian operation which led to the destruction of Germany's armies in France and the successful invasion of Germany in 1945.

While the British and Americans generally cooperated, there were disagreements about strategy with the British arguing for a more concentrated drive on the industrial Ruhr in the autumn of 1944 and Eisenhower wanting a more balanced broad-front strategy. In the end the USA's view prevailed as by 1945 it was the stronger partner.

# The Cold War and Relations with the USSR and China, 1945-72

## *The Breakdown of the Wartime Alliance, 1945-49*

### *Long-term Issues*

Both the USA and Britain had a chequered history of relations with the USSR since the revolution of 1917, based on the former's fears of the spread of communist revolution and the USSR's suspicion of capitalism. Western perceptions of the USSR had not been improved by:

* The Nazi-Soviet Pact, 1939  
* The USSR's part in the attack on and occupation of Poland, also in 1939  
* The USSR's attack on Finland in 1940-41 and the annexation of the Baltic states

The USSR, on the other hand, had been suspicious of the policy of appeasement pursued by Britain and France towards the fascist dictators.

### *The Fragile Alliance, 1941-45*

The German invasion of the USSR in 1941 temporarily buried these differences. Roosevelt cultivated a personal dialogue with Stalin, hoping that such diplomacy would improve relationships. Roosevelt's priorities at this stage were winning the war by sustaining the USSR, achieving Stalin's support for the idea of a post-war United Nations and persuading Stalin to join the war against Japan.

Despite arguments about the timing of a second front, the wartime alliance between Britain, the USA and the USSR held firm until 1945: Germany was defeated and conferences between Britain, the USA and the USSR continued to plan strategy in the war and to make plans for post-war Europe.

### *Yalta and Potsdam, 1945*

The three main leaders, Churchill, Roosevelt and Stalin, met at Yalta in February 1945. The outcome of the fighting meant that the Soviet army had occupied practically all of eastern Europe apart from Greece, which was occupied by Britain.

Of great concern to the western allies were the principles of self-determination and democracy in eastern European countries enshrined in the Atlantic Charter — these principles were to collide with the reality of Soviet military occupation of eastern Europe and Soviet concerns about its future security. The USSR did not want anti-communist states on its doorstep after the war.

Roosevelt was concerned at Yalta to ensure Soviet support for the United Nations and to secure its entry into the war against Japan. Poland was also a concern as it was the original reason Britain had gone to war, but for Roosevelt it was of less importance and he accepted a verbal assurance from Stalin that free elections would be held in Poland after the war.

On 12 April 1945 Roosevelt died and was replaced by his far less experienced vice president, Harry S. Truman. Truman was more inclined to be abrasive and in a strong exchange with Molotov, the Soviet foreign minister, on 23 April 1945 he demanded free elections in Poland when he believed the Soviets were dragging their feet about the issue.

However, the Soviets remained obdurate on Poland, remarking that they had allowed the western allies a free hand in Italy and Greece and questioning why they could not be allowed a similar role in Poland, which was vital for Russian security.

Against this background the Allied leaders met again at Potsdam in July 1945. There was agreement that Germany would be divided into four zones administered by each of the Allies (Britain, the USA, the USSR and France) with Berlin in the Soviet eastern zone also divided into four sectors. The Oder-Neisse line was accepted as Poland's new western frontier, but the long-term future of Germany was not agreed.

### *Truman's Options, 1945-46*

The Japanese war came to an end in August 1945 with the use of the atomic bomb. The outcome of Potsdam had confirmed in Truman's mind that the USSR would only respond to a tough line. America's possession of the atomic bomb and its economic power would, he hoped, enable him to pressurise the USSR into agreement on eastern Europe.

Truman was also aware of constraints on his own actions which would make a direct confrontation with the USSR undesirable:

* The American military had been rapidly demobilised — by March 1946 the American army was only 400,000 strong, down from a peak of 3.5 million in May 1945. It was estimated that the Red Army still had 2.5 million soldiers in 1946.  
* In 1946 the USA had very few atomic weapons, certainly not enough to inflict a decisive defeat on the USSR. In fact, it would not be long before the USSR developed its own atomic weapons.  
* Mid-term elections had given control of Congress to the Republicans, who had reverted to isolationism and also the reduction of government (and therefore military) expenditure.  
* It was unlikely that American public opinion would support a new conflict so soon after the Second World War.

However, Stalin and Molotov were deeply suspicious of American motives, seeing the offer of financial aid as a capitalist plot to undermine communism and their control of eastern Europe. They also refused to be intimidated by America's possession of an atomic bomb and maintained significant conventional forces in eastern Europe.

### *Kennan's Long Telegram*

In February 1946 George Kennan, an American diplomat in Moscow, wrote a 7,000-word memorandum which analysed Soviet policy. He believed that the USSR was so insecure that not only did it want to dominate eastern Europe, it would also use every tactic to undermine non-communist governments in western Europe. Kennan's '*long telegram*' powerfully influenced the Truman administration.

### *Truman Doctrine*

Concerns were raised over the presence of Soviet troops on the Turkish border and continuing Soviet demands for access to the Dardanelles Straits. A civil war was still in progress in Greece between communists and royalists (supported by British troops). Britain's parlous economic situation led to the announcement in February 1947 that it could no longer afford the Greek commitment.

Concerned that the strategic threat of a Soviet takeover of both Greece and Turkey should be countered, Truman went to Congress to demand military and economic aid for both countries. Mindful of the political composition of Congress and its attitude towards increased expenditure, Truman deliberately talked up the Soviet threat. In what became known as the **Truman Doctrine** he explained:

*I believe that it must be the policy of the US to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures.*

In May 1947, Congress agreed to $400 million aid for Greece and Turkey. The Truman Doctrine was an open-ended commitment and the basis of American foreign policy for at least the next 30 years.

### *Marshall Aid*

The newly appointed secretary of state, George Marshall, had just returned from a visit to Europe where he had been appalled at the extent of the economic and social distress. Nearly all the western European countries were suffering from economic problems; Britain had exhausted all its financial reserves by August 1947.

Marshall was afraid that an economic slump might result, affecting the USA and providing fertile ground in Europe for the spread of communism. He proposed an enormous package of financial aid known as **Marshall Aid** to rescue the European economies: the package was offered to all European countries including the USSR.

This was a shrewd move as it put the Soviet Union on the defensive. The Soviet rejection of Marshall Aid on the grounds that it was a capitalist ploy and an interference in the planned economies of eastern Europe was predictable. Between 1948 and 1952 Marshall Aid delivered $13.5 billion to 16 countries, the largest allocations going to Britain, France and Italy:

* The programme had an enormous impact on the recovery of the western European economies.  
* The aid to France and Italy probably did stave off the internal threat from communist parties.  
* The Soviet Union lost credibility in its handling of Marshall Aid.

Any doubts that Congress might have had were removed by the communist seizure of power in Czechoslovakia and the murder of its non-communist foreign minister Jan Masaryk in 1948.

### *Berlin Blockade*

Tensions were heightened by disagreements over Germany:

* The catalyst was the decision of the British and Americans to switch from seeking reparations from Germany to offering financial aid to Germany, and to fuse their separate zones (agreed at Potsdam) into one unit.  
* The supply of Marshall Aid to the new zone was a clear indication that the western powers were determined to integrate western Germany into a new country.  
* To the Soviets this was a betrayal of Potsdam and a further threat to their security.  
* The decision of the British and Americans in June 1948 to introduce a new currency, the Deutschmark, in their zone was the last straw.

The Soviets responded by closing off all the land routes to Berlin in the eastern zone. The presence of a relatively prosperous western enclave in the eastern zone was an embarrassment and the blockade was designed to force the western allies out of Berlin. Truman was determined not to back down and authorised an airlift to keep 2 million West Berliners supplied with basic food and fuel for 11 months.

The weak British and American land forces had no realistic prospect of resisting a Soviet attack using conventional forces. Large B29 bombers capable of delivering atomic bombs to targets in the USSR were therefore deployed to bases in Britain and the Middle East to send a signal to Stalin.

In May 1949 Stalin called off the blockade, which was not only ineffective but also a public relations disaster for the USSR. However, Berlin remained a source of confrontation in what was increasingly seen as a Cold War — hostility without shots being fired. A new state of West Germany was created in 1949, with a capital in Bonn, under the protection of the western allies. A rival East German state — the German Democratic Republic (GDR) — under Soviet control completed the division of Germany.

### *NATO and NSC-68*

The Berlin crisis had revealed the lack of western military preparedness. Isolationist sentiment was now in full retreat.

In April 1949 the North Atlantic Treaty Organisation (**NATO**) involving the USA, Canada and most west European countries was created, providing for mutual defence in the case of an attack. Congress also approved the extension of direct US military aid to NATO countries to build up their defences.

The Truman Doctrine and the establishment of NATO led to a fundamental re-evaluation of American foreign policy. Encapsulated in **NSC-68** (National Security Council Paper Number 68), this advocated a tripling of the American defence budget to meet what was perceived as a major military threat to the USA from the USSR and its allies. Truman had already authorised the development of an even more powerful nuclear weapon — the hydrogen bomb — in 1950.

### *Main Themes Affecting US Foreign Policy, 1945-50*

* The wartime alliance did not long survive the defeat of the Axis powers due in part to longstanding ideological differences.  
* The USA and USSR were suspicious of each other's motives, as shown by the Kennan telegram of 1946 and the USSR's rejection of American financial aid.  
* Soviet concerns over security were portrayed as an attempt to dominate the European continent while US attempts to support western economies were seen as an ill-disguised capitalist takeover.  
* Arguments about bad faith gained ground on both sides — the western allies saw Soviet actions in eastern Europe as violations of the Yalta agreement, while British and American actions in Germany were read as breaches of Potsdam by the USSR.  
* Stalin was a brutal and paranoid dictator, suspicious of others at home and abroad.  
* By 1949 relations between the USA and the USSR had deteriorated so much that the USA had for the first time in its peacetime history entered into a binding political and military agreement (NATO) with other countries, a complete reversal of isolationism.  
* The USA was committed to a policy of containment against communism and was seen as the leading power in the non-communist world. This resulted in some commitments to dubious governments whose only advantage was anti-communism.  
* Marshall Aid and the reconstruction of Japan were enormous peacetime financial commitments designed to arrest the spread of communism.

## *What Was the Impact of China Becoming a Communist Country in 1949?*

### *Background*

China had been divided by a civil war between the nationalists led by Chiang Kai-shek and the communists led by Mao Zedong since 1927. When the Japanese attacked and invaded China in 1937, both groups united in uneasy opposition to the invader.

China was considered economically important by the USA and Roosevelt's policy towards the Japanese was mainly designed to protect China. Roosevelt recognised Chiang Kai-shek as the most significant Chinese leader and was keen for China to be a leading member of the United Nations after the war.

Under Truman, America's policy continued to be supportive of the nationalists and Truman sent George Marshall on a peace mission in 1946 to encourage a settlement between the nationalists and the communists. A fragile truce was negotiated but it broke down because:

* Chiang Kai-shek adopted a more aggressive policy towards the communists, believing he would be supported.  
* When Soviet troops were withdrawn from Manchuria (they had captured it from the Japanese in the last week of the Second World War), fighting broke out between the nationalists and communists over who should control it.

### *Why Was There a Communist Victory in China by 1949?*

The fighting resulted in a communist victory by 1949:

* Although American aid was sent to the nationalists, it proved insufficient.  
* The crucial years of the civil war, 1947-49, were a time when the Truman administration was committed to its containment and aid policies in Europe. At this point it was beyond the strength of the USA to sustain containment in both Europe and Asia.  
* Mao gained wider support, particularly from the peasantry with the promise of land reforms.  
* In contrast, Chiang Kai-shek's government was notoriously corrupt, inefficient and increasingly ineffective. Its economic policies caused immense damage to savings and businesses.

By 1949 Mao's communist forces had won and Chiang's remaining supporters fled to the island of Taiwan. The Americans refused to recognise the communist government of China.

### *What Was the Impact on American Foreign Policy?*

Mao's success had a significant impact on US policy:

* The communist seizure of power was widely seen as a defeat in the USA.  
* It was thought that Mao's communists were mere puppets of the USSR and that communist China and the USSR were intent on world domination. The signing of a defence pact in 1950 between the USSR and China seemed to confirm this.  
* What was less apparent was the lukewarm support Stalin had given to Mao's communists. Stalin was in fact not overly keen on the creation of a large potentially independent communist power.  
* The outcome in China encouraged the rise of a new force in American politics — **McCarthyism**. Senator Joseph McCarthy publicly blamed the spread of communism on traitors in the US government.  
* McCarthyism popularised the idea that all that was required was to hunt out secret communists in the government. Expensive financial and military commitments were therefore not required. McCarthyism remained a force in US politics for several years and one that Truman could not ignore.

## *The Korean War*

### *Background*

Japan had occupied Korea since 1910. When the Second World War finished, the USA and USSR occupied the Korean peninsula, their zones divided by the 36th parallel of latitude. The intention was to unite Korea under the supervision of the newly created United Nations (UN) once elections were held.

Although the USA and USSR withdrew their troops, they each continued to provide military aid to their respective zones. In the northern zone the communist party was led by Kim Il Sung while in the southern zone a conservative leader, Syngman Rhee, emerged. The north refused to take part in the planned elections while the election in the south gave an uncertain victory to Rhee. Both Kim Il Sung and Syngman Rhee regarded themselves as the true leader of Korea.

### *Why Did Conflict Break Out in Korea?*

On 25 June 1950 the forces of Kim Il Sung launched a full-scale invasion of the south. This came as an unpleasant shock to Truman and the USA:

* Kim Il Sung had received the backing of Stalin for his action. Stalin's motivation may have been an attempt to divert US attention from Europe.  
* Truman's reaction was swift. He could not afford to appear weak after the communist success in China and the prominence of McCarthyism at home. He obtained UN resolutions condemning North Korean aggression and calling on UN members to assist South Korea. The Soviets were unable to use their veto on the Security Council as at the time they were boycotting the UN for its failure to recognise the new communist government of China.

### *The War*

The North Korean advance almost succeeded and at one point the UN and South Korean forces were boxed into the southeast corner of Korea at Pusan. However, in September 1950 MacArthur launched a daring amphibious counterattack at Inchon, routing the North Koreans, who were forced into headlong retreat.

The spectacular reversal of fortunes created a mood of over-confidence. Truman authorised MacArthur's forces to cross the 38th parallel and invade the north to settle the Korean issue permanently and unite the peninsula. Although UN approval was given to the move, this was a significant departure from containment — the USA and its allies had now embarked upon a war of liberation.

The Chinese were now thoroughly alarmed as the US forces advanced to the Yalu river, the border between Korea and China. China counterattacked across the Yalu river in January 1951, employing large armies against MacArthur's overstretched and ineptly deployed troops. The Chinese advance was unstoppable and the UN forces were driven back south of the 38th parallel.

MacArthur wanted to bomb China, if necessary using atomic weapons, but the American chiefs of staff were actually against extending the war to China. Using massively reinforced conventional forces, the UN fought back to the 38th parallel by March 1951.

At this point MacArthur exceeded his authority and demanded unconditional surrender from the Chinese, once again raising the issue of bombing China. Truman made the brave decision to sack MacArthur who was very popular and was given a hero's welcome on his return to the USA. However, Truman had asserted an important principle, that the president was commander-in-chief and that the military would be under civilian control.

Meanwhile the war reached a stalemate around the 38th parallel and negotiations began. Both sides were war weary and American casualties had been heavy with over 54,000 servicemen killed. Stalin's death in 1953 relaxed tensions and a new American president, Dwight Eisenhower, pressured the Chinese into further talks using the threat of nuclear warfare. An armistice but not a peace treaty was eventually agreed, with the border between north and south once again fixed at the 38th parallel where it has remained ever since.

### *What Was the Impact of the Korean War?*

The impact of the Korean War was as follows:

* Truman's actions in Korea had gone further than containment of communism: the USA sought to eliminate it in North Korea with disastrous consequences. Future US governments would be cautious about similar attempts to liberate communist countries.  
* There was a massive expansion of the American defence budget, which made possible the creation of the hydrogen bomb, new bases in the Middle and Far East, the doubling in size of the American air force and significantly the deployment of American forces in Europe. In 1950-51 NATO was given a further injection of military aid and an American army was now deployed in Europe. West Germany was eventually admitted to NATO in 1955 and was allowed to contribute military forces.  
* Chiang Kai-shek's outpost in Taiwan was given much more financial and military support.

# The Cold War and Relations with the USSR and China, 1945-72

## *The Korean War Aftermath and McCarthyism*

From 1950 to 1953 **McCarthyism** generated a hysterical witch hunt against suspected traitors in government. Although McCarthy was subsequently discredited when he over-reached himself in accusing the US army of subversion, he did prevent Truman from recognising communist China.

* The USA was now committed to a **containment** policy in both Europe and Asia.  
* The confrontation with China in Korea would impact directly on future US policies in Asia, particularly in Vietnam.

## *The Cold War and the Eisenhower Presidency, 1953-61*

Stalin's death in 1953 and his replacement by Khrushchev did signal a temporary thaw in the Cold War. Eisenhower was very concerned about the high level of American expenditure on defence and believed in personal diplomacy to resolve sources of conflict. This resulted in:

* American over-reliance on nuclear weapons, where the USA had overwhelming superiority  
* Eisenhower attempting a closer relationship with the USSR in the summit at Geneva in 1955-56. Although nothing concrete was achieved, the lines of communication were open.

Nevertheless, Cold War tensions were difficult to reduce:

* The Soviet suppression of the Hungarian uprising in 1956 was brutal and illustrated the Soviet grip on eastern Europe. The USA did not intervene; with reluctance it recognised that Hungary was within the USSR's sphere of influence.  
* Berlin continued to be a running sore in the relationship between the USSR and the West. A constant migration of people from East Germany to the western sector in Berlin increased Soviet pressure on the West to evacuate the city.  
* A further conference in Paris in 1960 between Eisenhower and Khrushchev was wrecked by the U2 incident. An American U2 spy plane had been shot down over Russia. American denials were exposed when the Soviets produced the pilot, Gary Powers. Relations between the two powers deteriorated.  
* In 1957 the USA was shocked by the launch of *Sputnik*, the world's first satellite, by the USSR. The perceived complacency of the Eisenhower administration was to become a major issue in the 1960 presidential election.  
* In the Far East the USA's relations with communist China were equally poor. The policy of support for Chiang Kai-shek and Taiwan led to tensions over the islands of Quemoy and Matsu. **SEATO** was set up in 1954 to mirror the NATO agreement in South East Asia.

## *The Kennedy Administration, 1961-63: Crises about Berlin and Cuba*

John F. Kennedy narrowly won the 1960 presidential election. Part of his election campaign had claimed that the Eisenhower administration had been complacent about the USSR's growing technological capability and that the USSR was rapidly closing the nuclear missile gap. In fact, the USA still had overwhelming nuclear capability, but Eisenhower could not reveal the evidence from the secret U2 spy plane flights which had taken place since 1956.

Kennedy was to replace Eisenhower's caution with a more active approach to foreign policy. In a stirring inaugural address, he promised that the USA would '*pay any price, bear any burden, meet any hardship, support any friend, oppose any foe to assure the survival and success of liberty*'.

Kennedy's handling of his first major crisis proved a disaster. In 1959 Fidel Castro had overthrown the Batista dictatorship in Cuba, setting up a one-party communist state and nationalising US companies. US sanctions followed and Castro sought Russian aid and assistance. The Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) planned an invasion of anti-Castro forces to destabilise the Castro government. The invasion, launched in April 1961 at the Bay of Pigs with Kennedy's backing, proved a complete failure. Khrushchev drew the conclusion that Kennedy was an inexperienced bungler.

### *Berlin*

Khrushchev was under some pressure to live up to his long history of tough talk about Berlin. Khrushchev met Kennedy at the Vienna conference in June 1961 and threatened him with a six months' ultimatum for the western allies to leave Berlin. This face-to-face meeting convinced Khrushchev that Kennedy was weak.

As a potential confrontation loomed, Khrushchev came up with a brutal solution. On 13 August 1961 he gave orders for the construction of a wall to separate East and West Berlin — access was now restricted to a few very tightly controlled checkpoints. Unusually designed to keep people in rather than out, the wall, for all the human tragedy it caused, did ease tensions between the USA and the USSR:

* As far as Kennedy was concerned, West Berlin stayed under western control and a wall was not worth fighting over.  
* Khrushchev had solved the immediate problem of the flow of refugees from the east.  
* However, the wall remained a stark symbol and reminder of the different ideologies fuelling the Cold War.

### *Cuban Missile Crisis*

A much more serious crisis erupted in October 1962 when American spy planes detected a launching pad and **medium-range ballistic missiles** supervised by Soviet officers on the island of Cuba.

Khrushchev's motivation in allowing the deployment of the missiles was influenced by:

* his frustration at not removing the western allies from Berlin  
* his need for a foreign policy success to fend off critics at home  
* increasing criticism from China about his policies  
* his impulsive style, which probably contributed to the crisis — the previous year in August 1961 he had boasted to the world about a nuclear weapon test that had exploded a 58-megaton hydrogen bomb, 3,000 times more powerful than the Hiroshima bomb and larger than anything the US possessed  
* his underestimation of Kennedy after the Bay of Pigs and the meeting in Vienna

Kennedy could not ignore the deployment of ballistic missiles so close to home; neither could he ignore the secrecy of the Soviet operation. His vigorous response surprised the USSR.

Kennedy made preparations for an invasion and a gradual, controlled response which would allow Khrushchev to back down without being completely humiliated.

On 22 October, Kennedy announced a quarantine around Cuba to be enforced by a naval blockade, appealing for Khrushchev to remove the missiles. NATO prepared for nuclear war as Soviet ships approached the blockade and on the 27th an American U2 plane was shot down over Cuba.

Khrushchev had sent a message on the 26th offering to withdraw the weapons if Kennedy undertook to remove the blockade and promise not to invade Cuba. A second message on the 27th, demanding the removal of American missiles from Turkey, was less conciliatory, reflecting perhaps Khrushchev's mercurial personality and the pressure he was under from his own military. Fidel Castro was also pressing for the Soviets to take action — if necessary, nuclear action.

The possibility of a nuclear war hung in the balance. It was decided that the president should reply to the first letter while behind the scenes he gave an assurance to the Soviet ambassador that the American missiles would be removed from Turkey.

The Soviet ships stopped short of the blockade and Khrushchev announced the withdrawal of missiles from Cuba on 28 October. The crisis was over with the sober realisation on both sides that they had come close to a nuclear war. While both sides had veered towards compromise, it was not clear at times that this would be the result. The risk of a war starting by accident was revealed later by a Soviet submarine commander who did consider releasing a nuclear torpedo on his own initiative when he was harassed by US ships near the blockade.

It was the most dangerous moment in the Cold War.

## *Consequences of the Cuban Missile Crisis*

The consequences of the Cuban Missile Crisis were:

* Kennedy gained significant prestige for his measured response to a dangerous crisis and the successful outcome of his policy. The success of a graduated response was to influence the direction of American policy in Vietnam.  
* What was missed in this analysis is that, although Khrushchev had backed down, Castro had not. Revolutionary nationalism had not been overawed by US power. It was a lesson that had to be learned anew in Vietnam.  
* Cuba's communist regime was now secure as the invasion threat from the USA had receded.  
* The threat of an accidental nuclear war persuaded the antagonists to set up a *hot line* — a direct communication channel between Washington and Moscow. In 1963 the three main nuclear powers, the USSR, the USA and Britain, agreed to a Limited Test Ban Treaty ending tests of nuclear weapons in the atmosphere.  
* In the USSR Khrushchev's colleagues had tired of his erratic policy and his lack of success in both Berlin and Cuba. He was removed from power in October 1964. Not wishing ever again to be placed in a position of nuclear inferiority, the USSR embarked upon a rapid expansion of its nuclear forces. By 1970 it had built, at great cost to its economy, more **inter-continental ballistic missiles** (ICBMs) than the USA.

It is important to understand how miscalculation and a misreading of a situation can quickly escalate into a crisis, as it did in 1962. Both Kennedy and Khrushchev had to consider conflicting advice as they stumbled towards a compromise, while on the ground events (e.g. the shooting down of the U2 plane or the temporary panic of the Soviet submarine commander) could quickly have triggered hostilities.

## *Redirection of US Policy Towards the Cold War, 1963-72*

Much of American focus during this period was on the Vietnam War. However, there was a noticeable relaxation of tension between the USSR and the USA, a process that is given the shorthand term '*détente*':

* To some extent the process of détente was inspired by more independent policies from NATO allies. In West Germany during the 1960s Willy Brandt, a prominent politician, popularised the idea of *Ostpolitik*, reducing tensions with the USSR in the hope that eventually Germany might be peacefully united.  
* The French president, Charles de Gaulle, who resented US dominance of NATO, struck a more independent line, leaving NATO in 1966 and adopting his own policy towards the USSR.  
* However, it was largely the cost of maintaining large nuclear arsenals and the potential dangers of nuclear war that brought about renewed talks between the USA and USSR to reduce nuclear weapons.

The redirection of US policy was clearly seen during the presidency of Richard Nixon (1969-74) with policies of détente towards both the USSR and China. Nixon's national security advisor (and from 1973, the secretary of state) **Henry Kissinger** played a significant role in fostering personal links — *shuttle diplomacy* with world leaders, carrying out much of the preparatory work underpinning détente.

This bore fruit in the 1972 **SALT** treaty agreed at a summit meeting in Moscow, which placed limits on the number of inter-continental and submarine-launched ballistic missiles (SLBMs) held by the USA and USSR. However, SALT left out many categories of weapons and was limited to five years.

Of greater significance was Nixon's policy towards China:

* Aware of the growing hostility between the USSR and China (there had been border clashes in 1969) and China's influence on North Vietnam, Nixon sought to divide the main communist powers and to enlist the support of the Chinese in ending the war in Vietnam.

## *Nixon's Policy of Détente with China*

There were also economic motives, as China would be a market for American goods at a time when the US economy was under pressure because of the Vietnam War. Kissinger's visit to Beijing in 1971 heralded the thaw in relations.

Nixon became the first American president to visit communist China in a well-publicised visit in 1972 when he met Mao and his premier Zhou Enlai.

Although full diplomatic relations were not restored for six years, trade increased and, significantly, communist China was granted a seat on the Security Council of the UN.

## *The Vietnam War*

### *The Problem of Vietnam*

Before the Second World War, Vietnam was part of French Indo-China. During the war it was occupied by the Japanese. A group of Vietnamese nationalists, the Vietminh, waged a guerrilla war against the Japanese. The most powerful group within the Vietminh was the communist party led by Ho Chi Minh. When the Japanese were defeated in 1945, Ho proclaimed Vietnamese independence from France and established a government in Hanoi.

The French, however, refused to grant independence and sent troops to Vietnam. Ho's protestations that this was a violation of the Atlantic Charter counted for little in the postwar atmosphere of containment. The Vietminh were regarded as part of the Soviet plan for world domination and a French-backed pro-western government was set up in Saigon. In 1954 the French army was defeated at Dien Bien Phu by Ho Chi Minh's forces.

During the thaw in relations between the USA and USSR after the death of Stalin, the major powers met at Geneva in 1954-55 to resolve the Vietnam issue. It was agreed that:

* The French would leave Vietnam.  
* Vietnam would be divided at the 17th parallel, with a demilitarised zone (DMZ) temporarily separating the south and north.  
* An election was promised to decide whether the two zones would be united.

Afraid that Ho would actually win the election, the USA refused to hold it, supporting the leader of the south, Ngo Dinh Diem. Eisenhower committed the USA to defending the south, endorsing the '*domino theory*' (that if Vietnam fell under communist control, other countries in the region would topple like a line of dominoes).

Diem's government was unpopular and corrupt. A Catholic in a country where most of his people were Buddhist, Diem struggled to establish a legitimate government. South Vietnamese communist guerrillas, the Vietcong, supported militarily by North Vietnam, began a campaign against Diem's government. The South Vietnamese army struggled to make any headway against the Vietcong and North Vietnamese forces.

### *Why Did the USA Intervene?*

Underestimating the power of nationalism and interpreting a civil war as part of the Cold War, successive US governments escalated US involvement in Vietnam:

* The Eisenhower administration sent both financial aid and military aid to South Vietnam.  
* This assistance was significantly increased by the Kennedy administration. As a strong supporter of containment, Kennedy sent special forces to mount covert operations in North Vietnam and by 1963 had committed 16,300 military advisers to the South. Concerned about the unpopularity and corruption of the Diem government, he authorised a military coup against Diem who was assassinated in November 1963. He was replaced by military leaders who lacked popular support. Within three weeks Kennedy too had been assassinated.  
* Kennedy's successor, Lyndon Johnson, went further. On 2 August 1964 it was alleged that North Vietnamese gunboats had fired on US destroyers in the Gulf of Tonkin. Although the circumstances of the incident were unclear, Johnson asked for, and received, a resolution from Congress, known as the Gulf of Tonkin resolution, which authorised the president to take 'all necessary measures to repel any armed attack against the armed forces of the United States and to prevent further aggression'. Johnson authorised US air attacks on North Vietnam and the commitment of ground troops in 1965.  
* Operation Rolling Thunder — the bombing of North Vietnamese targets — began in March 1965 and became the largest aerial bombardment in history. Six million tons of high explosive (twice that of the Second World War) were dropped by US aircraft. By 1969, 536,600 US troops had also been committed to Vietnam. The total US expenditure on the war has been estimated at a staggering $106.8 billion, which dwarfed Johnson's Great Society programme.  
* This escalation was the consequence of the containment policy followed by all US governments since 1947. To abandon Vietnam and see its inevitable unification under Ho Chi Minh's communist rule would have been contrary to the direction of US policy to that date.  
* It was thought that the USA's credibility as a world power was at stake — if it crumbled in Vietnam, what message would this give to its friends and allies in the rest of the world?  
* Most of the key advisers in both the Kennedy and Johnson administrations had been young men at the time of the Second World War and nearly all had seen active service. The memory of appeasement and its consequences had been indelibly printed on their minds during their formative years. It should be no surprise that this would inform their policies as politicians.  
* Once the commitment had been made, it would have been seen as politically disastrous for a US president to back down.  
* In 1965 the top US advisers nearly all believed that the commitment of US air power and ground forces would tip the balance and that a gradual, escalated response would bring North Vietnam to the negotiation table and bolster the South Vietnamese government.

### *Why Was American Intervention Unsuccessful?*

US involvement in Vietnam was unsuccessful for the following reasons:

* American military power proved ineffective. It relied on a gradual escalation of bombing with 'search and destroy' missions launched from fortified bases by US troops designed to inflict heavy casualties on the Vietcong and the North Vietnamese army. In January 1968 the North Vietnamese launched a massive counterattack — the *Tet Offensive* — aimed at most urban areas in South Vietnam. Although repulsed with heavy casualties (it was a tactical disaster for the Vietcong and North Vietnamese), its psychological impact on the US government's will to continue and the American public's trust in its government was devastating.  
* The Vietcong and North Vietnamese had many advantages. After a defeat they could easily regroup in safer areas outside South Vietnam, supplied by the Ho Chi Minh trail through Cambodia. The geography of Vietnam lent itself to guerrilla operations, with the cover of the jungle and the support of peasant villages. The North Vietnamese leaders, Ho Chi Minh and General Giap, had already been successful against the French and had the capacity and willpower to engage patiently in a long-drawn-out struggle and accept heavy casualties. American public opinion would come to accept neither.  
* In contrast, the performance of the South Vietnamese army was generally inadequate and lacked popular support and credibility.  
* American saturation bombing, the use of napalm and the destruction of villages caused untold destruction and misery. Civilian deaths, north and south, ran into millions. The strategy did not win 'hearts and minds'.  
* After the Tet Offensive, General Westmoreland asked for a further 200,000 troops: this was politically unacceptable in 1968. Johnson could not extend the war into North Vietnam because (with Korea in mind) he could not risk the involvement of the USSR and China. The use of nuclear weapons was not a realistic option for the same reason.  
* The long-drawn-out guerrilla campaign sapped the morale of US forces, many of whom were beginning to doubt why they were fighting in Vietnam by the late 1960s. Discipline was undermined by the widespread availability of drugs.  
* By late 1967 an anti-war movement had gathered momentum, particularly at universities and in draft avoidance. Coverage of the war on US media regularly showed disturbing images and reporting, which undermined support for continued fighting. The high death toll — at least 30,000 Americans killed by 1968 — fed into political opposition.  
* Johnson faced significant political opposition from within his own party by 1968, with both Senator McCarthy and Senator Robert Kennedy openly opposing the war and running successfully in the Democrat primaries.  
* A demoralised Johnson announced a halt to aerial bombardment on 31 March 1968 and stunned the nation by declaring that he would not seek re-election as president. Vietnam had destroyed him. Preliminary peace talks with Hanoi started the following month.

### *The War Drags On, 1969-75*

Johnson's successor, Richard Nixon, pledged to end the war. Recognising that further American involvement was politically impossible, he decided upon:

* the policy of *Vietnamisation*, whereby the South Vietnamese army would be supported and trained to replace the US army  
* pressure on North Vietnam, if necessary by further bombing, to make an acceptable peace — the peace talks that began in 1968 had stalled  
* the expansion of the war into Cambodia to disrupt the offensive capacity of the North Vietnamese by destroying their guerrilla bases

Nixon's policies did reduce the number of American troops, so that by September 1972 there were only 40,000 left in Vietnam. Nevertheless, his expansion of the war into Cambodia increased the already high casualty rates. Nixon had secretly ordered the bombing of North Vietnamese bases in Cambodia in 1969, only publicly admitting the action in April 1970.

This triggered even more opposition to the war:

* Four students were shot dead by national guardsmen at Kent State University in May 1971.  
* The My Lai massacre trial further inflamed public opinion: Lieutenant William Calley was convicted of the murder of 22 Vietnamese civilians.  
* The publication of the Pentagon Papers by the New York Times in the summer of 1971 revealed the scale of US government deception in starting the war, including misinformation about the Gulf of Tonkin incident.

When the North Vietnamese launched a new military offensive against a weakened south in May 1972, Nixon authorised a huge aerial bombardment of the north. An American concession allowing North Vietnamese forces to remain in the south restarted the talks. Final agreement was only reached in January 1973 after the resumption of bombing over Christmas 1972.

The peace agreed did permit withdrawal of American forces and the return of prisoners of war, but it was fundamentally flawed in leaving North Vietnamese forces in South Vietnam.

Although the south was promised financial and military support by Nixon, his domestic position was completely undermined by the Watergate scandal and the determination of Congress not to provide further aid to South Vietnam. It passed the War Powers Act in 1973, putting strict limits on the time an American president could keep troops abroad without the approval of Congress.

When the north renewed hostilities against a feeble south in 1974-75, the US Congress refused to provide further assistance to South Vietnam. Saigon fell to communist forces in April 1975 — American involvement in Vietnam had been a complete failure.

### *What Were the Consequences of US Failure?*

The consequences of US failure in Vietnam were considerable:

* Around 58,000 American servicemen were dead and the gargantuan cost of the war not only wrecked Johnson's Great Society programme, but also triggered inflation, undermining America's position as an economic superpower.  
* The suffering of the Vietnamese was much greater. Half a million South Vietnamese civilians were killed, hundreds of thousands were injured and 5 million became refugees. North Vietnamese military losses numbered half a million and civilian casualties may have reached into the millions. The human catastrophe was made worse by the destabilisation of Cambodia, resulting in the Khmer Rouge dictatorship which was responsible for millions of deaths.  
* At home, disillusionment set in partly because of Watergate and partly because it was now clear that the government had lied to its people about the escalation of the war, troop numbers and the bombing of Cambodia.

### **The Making of a Superpower, c.1890–1990**

The consensus around containment was in tatters, with most Americans confused and deeply divided about the direction of foreign policy. The domino theory was laid to rest. Its mythology about a united communist plan was soon shown to be baseless when, in 1976, communist Vietnam went to war with communist Cambodia; by 1978, communist China was also at war with communist Vietnam.

### **The Failure of Détente, 1975–80**

At first, the process of détente survived the downfall of Richard Nixon, who had resigned as president over the Watergate scandal in August 1974. His successor, Gerald Ford, met Leonid Brezhnev, the leader of the USSR, at Vladivostok in November 1974 and made an agreement to negotiate a follow-up to SALT with a further reduction of strategic nuclear weapons.

#### **Helsinki Accords**

The atmosphere of cooperation was strengthened in 1975 by the Helsinki Accords, an agreement between 35 countries including the USA, the USSR and most European countries. This included a significant acceptance of human rights and the 'peaceful transformation of borders'. The Helsinki Accords were to have long-term significance for the monitoring of human rights in the Soviet Union and eastern Europe, as well as the direction of US foreign policy under the next president, Jimmy Carter (1977–81).

#### **SALT II**

Carter promised to follow a more moral agenda in foreign policy, one that would be based on principle and human rights. Although negotiations with the USSR did proceed on SALT II, they were often fractious because of Carter's determination to link arms control with human rights issues. However, by 1979, outline agreement on SALT II had been achieved:

* A limit of 2,400 on nuclear delivery vehicles, falling to 2,250 by 1982  
* A limit on the number of warheads per vehicle

This was a limited agreement but it was one that, for the first time, proposed a reduction in, as well as a limit to, nuclear weaponry. The huge problem with SALT II was that it did not take into account a new factor in the nuclear equation — the deployment in 1976 and 1977 of Soviet medium-range nuclear missiles in Europe, the *SS20s*. As the debate to ratify SALT II was under way in Congress, the prospects for détente were undermined by:

* The USSR's achievement in securing nuclear parity. By 1975 the USSR had more nuclear delivery vehicles than the USA and between 1977 and 1983 it had exceeded the USA's number of nuclear warheads.  
* The USSR's deployment of SS20s in eastern Europe.  
* Soviet involvement in the Angolan civil war in 1975-76 and the Horn of Africa in 1978. By February 1978 there were 15,000 Cuban troops in Ethiopia and the USSR had given $1 billion in military aid.

Two events in 1979-80 finally shattered the prospects for détente and, consequently, the signing of SALT II:

* In 1979 the Iranian Islamic revolution removed the country's ruler, the Shah, from power. Iran under the Shah had been an important Cold War ally of the USA. In November 1979 followers of the new Iranian leader, the Ayatollah Khomeini, stormed the US embassy in Teheran and seized 63 American hostages. A rescue mission authorised by President Carter ended in ignominious failure in 1980.  
* In December 1979 the USSR invaded Afghanistan. This was the largest deployment of Soviet troops outside its territory since the Second World War. The USSR claimed it was a defensive move to secure its southern republics against Islamic fundamentalism. In the context of the other events of the late 1970s, President Carter saw it as a threat to peace from an aggressive USSR.

As a result President Carter ordered:

* the withdrawal of SALT II from the Senate  
* a boycott of the Olympic Games held in Moscow in 1980  
* a ban on the sale of grain and high-tech items to the USSR

Détente was over and a new era in the Cold War now began. Ronald Reagan, the Republican candidate for the presidency in 1980, criticised Carter's policies as weak and misguided, and portrayed the USSR as an aggressive, expansionist power that could only be curbed by confrontation and a massive expansion of America's military capability. He won by a landslide in the 1980 presidential election.

## *Reagan's Policy Towards the USSR, 1981-89*

The presidential election in 1980 brought a conservative anti-communist to the White House. Reagan was a strong believer that the USA should not only build up US military and economic power but also actively roll back the Soviet Union's influence in the world by increasing pressure on it at home.

### *The Reagan Doctrine*

This policy found expression in National Security Decision Directive (NSDD) 75, which was issued in 1983 and is sometimes known as the **Reagan Doctrine**. While this hard-line policy was seen by many as a deliberate escalation of the Cold War, less well publicised was the president's belief that, once in a position of strength, the USA could negotiate nuclear disarmament and resolve any outstanding disagreements with the USSR.

### *Reagan's Hard-line Policy Towards the USSR, 1981-83*

Reagan's hard-line policy towards the USSR was quickly revealed:

* He denounced the Soviet Union as '*an evil empire*' in a speech in March 1983.  
* A massive military aid programme was provided for Pakistan because it bordered Afghanistan, and Reagan also supported the Islamic *Mujahedeen* rebels in Afghanistan.  
* Economic sanctions were imposed against the USSR when martial law was declared in Poland in December 1981 to crack down on the dissident *Solidarity* trade union.  
* Anti-communist forces in Nicaragua and El Salvador were supported, despite UN reports about their atrocities and human rights abuses.  
* A huge expansion of the US defence budget was announced, one of the largest peacetime military programmes in US history. This was a five-year programme with a target of $1.6 trillion, including the development of the stealth bomber, a sophisticated MX missile, Trident submarines and a 33% increase in the size of the US navy. The most spectacular announcement came in March 1983 with the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI), nicknamed '*Star Wars*'. This envisaged a defensive missile shield which could intercept and destroy any incoming missiles. While there was some scepticism from experts about the feasibility of the project, the USSR's leadership believed that the USA was planning a *first-strike capability* in a bid to make a nuclear war winnable.  
* Reagan also strongly supported NATO's deployment of Pershing II missiles into Europe as a counter to the Soviet SS20s. There were huge demonstrations in western Europe against this deployment, reflecting the growing fears of nuclear war in an atmosphere of heightened tension between the USA and USSR.  
* In September 1983 relations between them reached a low point when Soviet air defences shot down a Korean (KAL) airliner which had strayed into Russian airspace.

### *The Change in Policy from 1984*

The hard-line approach that characterised the early years of the Reagan presidency had changed by 1984. There were several reasons for this:

* Reagan was personally alarmed by reports that the USSR had put its nuclear forces on high alert in November 1983 during a NATO exercise in Europe (Able Archer), which the Russians thought was preparatory to a first strike. Reagan would have been even more alarmed if he had known about an incident on 26 September 1983 when a defective computer had set off an ICBM alarm in a Soviet early warning centre. The action of Colonel Petrov in double checking before passing on the alert probably averted nuclear disaster.  
* A speech by Reagan in January 1984 signalled that he was willing to build a more constructive relationship with the USSR. There is little evidence that the Reagan administration was deliberately trying to bankrupt the USSR with its defence strategy — the priority was to defend the USA.  
* The USSR had undergone rapid changes in leadership. After Brezhnev's death in 1982, two frail successors, Andropov (1982-84) and Chernenko (1984-85), did not last long. The appointment of Mikhail Gorbachev in 1985 was to prove decisive in ending the Cold War. Gorbachev embarked on a reform agenda known as *glasnost* (openness) and *perestroika* (restructuring). He intended to preserve communism through reforms, revive a stagnant economy and reduce exorbitant expenditure on the military. He also intended to end the Soviet occupation of Afghanistan. Gorbachev appeared willing and able to embark on a new approach to relations with the USA.

Reagan and Gorbachev agreed to summit meetings at Geneva in November 1985 and Reykjavik in October 1986. The discussions went further than their respective advisers had expected, with talk of massive reductions in nuclear weapons. The two leaders cooperated well.

### *The End of the Cold War?*

Reagan's visit to Berlin in June 1987 enabled him to score a propaganda coup with a speech exhorting Gorbachev to '*tear down this wall*'. It was a signal that a reduction in arms was not the only objective of US policy.

The two leaders met again in Washington in December 1987 and this time the Intermediate Nuclear Force (INF) Treaty eliminated the Pershing IIs and SS20s, the first time a whole class of weapons systems had been abolished. The INF Treaty was ratified by the US Senate.

Further talks in Moscow in May 1988 included Reagan and Gorbachev's highly symbolic stroll in Red Square. There was no doubt that relations between the USA and USSR had been completely transformed:

* It has been argued that Reagan's massive expansion of US military power forced the Soviet Union to wreck its economy in order to compete. Others have argued that the hard-line rhetoric and excessive military expenditure delayed negotiations, alarming the Soviet Union and feeding its defensive mentality, as shown in the KAL disaster. Reagan's friend and ally Margaret Thatcher claimed that he had won the Cold War 'without firing a shot'.  
* Gorbachev's willingness to abandon the Cold War and embark on internal reforms in the USSR created a different atmosphere and forced the pace, but Reagan's decision to soften his Cold War rhetoric actually predated Gorbachev's appointment.  
* Reagan seized the opportunity presented by Gorbachev and showed himself to be a flexible negotiator after the shaky start of 1985-86.  
* Both leaders had the benefit of able and dedicated aides. George Shultz as Reagan's secretary of state and Edward Shevardnadze as Gorbachev's foreign minister had the capacity to forge productive relationships and negotiate complex arms agreements.

## *Impact of the Fall of Communism on American Foreign Policy, 1989-91*

### *The Collapse of Communism*

Early in 1989 Gorbachev ordered the withdrawal of Soviet troops from Afghanistan: their lack of success and the mounting costs made the Soviet presence unsustainable.

Events now moved with dizzying speed: in July 1989, in a speech at Strasbourg, Gorbachev renounced the *Brezhnev Doctrine*. The Soviet Union would no longer use force to keep control of eastern Europe. Communist regimes in eastern Europe crumbled.

In Poland, Solidarity was allowed to contest elections and in August 1989 a non-communist prime minister was appointed. A new government emerged in Hungary, while in East Germany the communist government collapsed.

Huge crowds of East Berliners demonstrated at the Berlin Wall and in November 1989 the border guards opened the barriers. The Berlin Wall, a potent symbol of the Cold War, was no more as 3 million East Germans visited West Berlin. The dissident writer Vaclav Havel was elected president of Czechoslovakia and a popular uprising in Romania overthrew the Ceausescu regime — the Ceausescu family were executed on Christmas Day 1989.

In the Soviet Union itself Gorbachev's reforms did not bring about his anticipated improvement in the Soviet economy. Instead chaos and disillusionment resulted. The Baltic states broke away, as did many of the Soviet Union's republics in a wave of nationalist revolts encouraged by events in eastern Europe. By the end of 1991 the USSR had been replaced by several independent states, the largest being Russia, the Communist Party had been dissolved and Gorbachev had resigned.

The new US president George H. Bush (1989-93) cautiously welcomed the events in eastern Europe and the USSR:

* In June 1990 he and Gorbachev met in Washington and negotiated cuts in chemical and nuclear weapons in a new treaty, *START I*.  
* American diplomacy played an important role in the reunification of Germany. An uneasy Gorbachev and doubtful western leaders such as Margaret Thatcher and François Mitterrand were persuaded by the Bush administration to support a united Germany. By October 1990 the armies of occupation were removed and Germany was reunited under the leadership of the new chancellor, Helmut Kohl. One of the largest issues in the Cold War had been resolved.

### *Implications of the Downfall of Communism*

The fall of communism had the following results:

* In 1990-91 the end of the Cold War and the breakup of the Soviet Union created what some have seen as a '*unipolar moment*', in the sense that the USA had no serious rival and faced no challenge to its status as an economic and political superpower.  
* While the USA's economic position was still very strong, the 1980s had seen a massive expansion of US debt. By 1990 the USA was the largest debtor nation in history and in an ironic twist its largest creditors at that time were Germany and Japan. West Germany was now the world's leading exporter of manufactured goods. Nevertheless, the US economy was still so large that once the Bush and Clinton administrations (1989-2001) had controlled and eliminated the federal debt, the US economy was still the most dominant in the world in 2000.
