# **19th century background to foreign policy**

During the nineteenth century the emergence of the USA as a significant world power had been strongly influenced by its isolation and secure position surrounded by large oceans and relatively weak neighbours. In 1796 America's first president, George Washington, had recommended that the USA should avoid alliances and an adventurous foreign policy

## *The Monroe Doctrine (1823)*

In 1823, when Spain's South American colonies were struggling for independence, President Monroe:

* ### ***declared** that the USA would not tolerate any intervention in the affairs of the Americas by European powers or any further colonisation*

* ### ***made it clear** that the USA would not interfere in Europe or any existing European colonies not in rebellion*

The importance of this public statement was *symbolic rather than real* at that time: America did not have the military power to enforce it. It was, in fact, British diplomacy and the power of the Royal Navy that prevented European intervention in South America. However, Monroe's announcement, known as the *Monroe Doctrine*, became an important influence on American foreign policy.

## *Manifest Destiny*

### *Of more importance in the nineteenth century was the westward expansion of the USA, summed up in the phrase **'Manifest Destiny'**. The phrase popularised the idea that the United States was intended to seize and control the whole North American continent. Neatly combining a justification for aggression against native Indians and Mexicans and a missionary drive to export US democracy and liberty, Manifest Destiny powered the absorption of huge western territories into the USA, notably:*

* Texas  
* California  
* Oregon (in the 1840s)  
* Later the vast Indian territories of the west

There was also a tradition of purchasing land from European powers: for example, Louisiana in 1803 and Alaska in 1867. Involvement in this continental expansion and recovery from the Civil War (1861-65) deterred the USA from significant involvement in international affairs.

## *Change in the Late Nineteenth Century*

By the late nineteenth century, however, this situation was changing:

* ### ***America's economy boomed** and the country rapidly industrialised, aided by its own abundant natural resources and a new railway network. Its GNP quadrupled from $9 billion in 1869-73 to $37 billion between 1897 and 1901. Around 30% of the world's manufactured goods were made in the USA by 1900. In a short space of time, the USA had become the leading industrial nation of the world and, as a result, second only to Britain as a major financial power. Economic considerations were now important, as the USA would need access to world markets.*

* ### ***Increased trade** meant that America would also need a stronger navy to protect its trade and overseas bases to protect its growing global interests. In 1885 America had a very small navy: by 1905 it had the third largest in the world behind Britain and Germany.*

* ### ***The late nineteenth century** saw a surge in European imperialism: the large European powers were busily expanding in Africa and Asia, and acquiring island bases in the major oceans. Influential Americans now questioned whether isolationism was in America's best interests. This was powerfully allied to the growing popularity of social Darwinism — if the USA did not expand, it would decline. Popular theories about the racial superiority of the Anglo-Saxon race held sway.*

* ### ***During the late nineteenth century**, America acquired new bases in the Pacific in Samoa and Hawaii. The annexation of Hawaii in 1898, including a naval base at Pearl Harbour, eventually went ahead after five years of argument in Congress about the morality of the venture. American foreign policy was becoming increasingly assertive.*

# The Spanish-American War and American Imperialism

Spanish colonies in Cuba and the Philippines became increasingly difficult to rule. Cuban independence fighters staged rebellions between 1868 and 1878. An American tariff on sugar in 1894 plunged the Cuban economy into chaos, prompting another rebellion against Spanish rule in 1895. There was strong support for the rebellion from the American public, politicians and newspapers, particularly Hearst's *New York Journal*.

In February 1898 an American battleship, USS *Maine*, blew up in Havana harbour, killing 260 American sailors. The explosion has never been satisfactorily explained but Spain was blamed. The American press whipped up public hysteria on the issue to such a pitch that, when Spain refused to give Cuba independence, President McKinley asked Congress to declare war in April 1898.

The war lasted for only four months. The USA was fortunate that Spain's antiquated navy and incompetently led army were no match for the modern ships of the US navy and the small but enthusiastic US army that invaded Cuba. At the battle of San Juan Hill, the success of a volunteer regiment led by Theodore Roosevelt made its leader a war hero.

A peace treaty signed in Paris in December 1898 recognised Cuban independence and the American annexation of the Philippines, Puerto Rico and the Pacific island of Guam. America had acquired an empire:

* The acquisition prompted a fierce debate about the morality of empire, not only in the Senate but also in the 1900 presidential election.  
* The annexation of the Philippines was especially controversial — was it against the spirit of the Declaration of Independence? Would it involve the USA in foreign conflict? Would it lead to huge expenditure?  
* Despite this there was a surge in national pride following the successful war. The commercial and strategic advantages of empire and the prevalence of racial arguments about the superiority of the white race won the day.

The Filipinos were not necessarily grateful, having exchanged Spanish rulers for Americans. A full-scale revolt against American rule began in February 1899 and lasted three years. It involved 70,000 American troops, far more than in the 1898 war, and the expenditure of $170 million. Like most guerrilla wars it involved brutality and savagery, claiming 4,200 American lives and thousands more Filipinos. Eventually American control was established, but the acquisition of the Philippines had borne out the worst fears of the anti-imperialists about the potential liabilities of empire.

# Theodore Roosevelt and the Meaning of US Imperialism

### *In 1901 President McKinley was assassinated and was succeeded by the vice president, the popular **Theodore Roosevelt**. Roosevelt's policies continued the expansionist trend in US foreign policy:*

* In 1903 the USA intervened in a dispute between Colombia and the inhabitants of Panama, resulting in the independence of Panama and a treaty favouring the USA. This entrusted the USA with the construction and operation of a canal joining the Pacific Ocean with the Caribbean, giving the USA enormous strategic and commercial advantages.  
* In Cuba independence was dominated by US influence and the country became, in effect, an economic colony of the USA. The Cubans were forced to accept the *Platt Amendment*, which gave the USA the right to intervene in Cuba if its independence or stable government was threatened. It also permitted the Americans to establish a permanent naval base in Guantanamo Bay.  
* American economic power also dominated Central America with US corporations such as the United Fruit and American Tobacco Companies dominating local economies.  
* The secretary of state John Hay announced an '*open door*' policy by which all foreign nations should have the same commercial access to China. He also stated that the USA would protect China's independence and territory.  
* US intervention was emphasised by Roosevelt's amendment of the Monroe Doctrine, known as the *Roosevelt Corollary*. This asserted that the USA could intervene in Caribbean countries, 'however reluctantly', if they were threatened externally or internally.  
* Roosevelt burnished his naval credentials by sending 16 US battleships on a 46,000-mile world publicity cruise in 1907. Nicknamed the '*Great White Fleet*', it marked the emergence of a powerful US navy.  
* Roosevelt also acted as a peacemaker, facilitating the Treaty of Portsmouth in the 1904-05 Russo-Japanese War and exercising some influence in the Algeciras conference in 1906 which averted war between France and Germany over the First Moroccan Crisis. The USA was now accepted as a major power.

# Woodrow Wilson and the Problem of Neutrality

### ***Woodrow Wilson** was elected the first Democrat president for twenty years in 1912. Idealistic, deeply religious and a firm believer in liberalism, he was keen to reverse what he saw as the aggressive policies of his predecessors. In October 1913 he announced that the USA 'would never again seek an additional foot of territory by conquest'.*

## *Latin America*

Under the pressure of events, however, Wilson's approach to Latin America appeared little different. Although dressed up in the language of liberal and moral diplomacy, his administrations intervened militarily once in Cuba, twice in Panama and five times in Honduras. Hispaniola and Haiti were occupied by American troops in 1915.

The Mexican revolution which had begun in 1911 tested Wilson's interpretation of the Roosevelt Corollary. Wilson's sympathies were with the liberal revolutionaries, so when a military dictator, General Huerta, seized power in 1913, Wilson was outraged. He authorised intervention which at one point involved the bombardment of Vera Cruz, killing 126 Mexicans. This intervention managed to upset both sides in the Mexican civil war. Wilson was lucky that other Latin American countries mediated, bringing the civil war to a temporary halt.

A year later it broke out again with one Mexican leader, Pancho Villa, attacking an American border town. Wilson had little choice but to order a punitive expedition in response. Several thousand US cavalry men unsuccessfully chased Pancho Villa around northern Mexico, withdrawing in January 1917 with little to show for their efforts except ruffled Mexican feelings.

The civil war ended in 1917 and Mexico did adopt a liberal constitution, which Wilson had wanted all along. However, Mexican national pride had been left deeply injured by his actions.

## *The First World War*

### *Neutrality?*

The outbreak of war in Europe in 1914 presented Wilson with far greater problems. In August 1914 he announced that the USA would be neutral. The scale of the conflict meant that this neutrality would be tested. American industry and agriculture received an enormous boost, supplying foodstuffs, raw materials and munitions to the warring powers. The USA was also willing to lend enormous sums to them. By 1917 they had received no less than $2 billion of war loans from the USA, of which Germany received only $27 million. Germany protested that these were not the acts of a neutral country.

However, US-British trade had always been on a large scale even before the war. In any case, making a loan to a country at war had never been considered a breach of neutrality and Britain's assets were sufficient to guarantee any loans.

### *Unrestricted Submarine Warfare*

Both sides in the war attempted to blockade the other. Britain's blockade was the traditional one of stopping neutral shipping, searching it and confiscating goods bound for Germany. Although this was annoying for the USA, Wilson chose not to make a big issue of it, partly because Germany's trade with the USA was comparatively small and partly because he was mindful that the 1812 war with Britain had been caused by disputes over a blockade.

However, in February 1915 Germany decided to adopt a new type of blockade using submarines. It announced that all enemy merchant ships entering a war zone around Britain would be sunk without warning: neutral ships should therefore avoid the war zone. Wilson sent a protest to Germany: the legal niceties of stop and search had been abandoned in favour of more ruthless methods.

On 7 May the passenger liner *Lusitania* was sunk in the war zone by a German submarine. It sank in 20 minutes, drowning 1,198 people, 128 of whom were American. The Germans claimed the ship had been carrying munitions. This was true but the American public and press were outraged, as indeed was Wilson.

His initial response that 'there is such a thing as being too proud to fight' emphasised his desire to remain neutral. Later he toughened his response and, after further sinkings, threatened to break off diplomatic relations with Germany. A year after the *Lusitania* disaster, in May 1916 Germany abandoned unrestricted submarine warfare.

### *Key Terms*

* ***Imperialism***: The drive, primarily by European powers, to acquire, administer and develop less advanced territories for prestige as well as economic and strategic reasons.  
* ***Social Darwinism***: A theory inspired by Charles Darwin's theory of evolution which asserted that human history should be seen as the survival of the fittest. It was particularly important in fostering ideas of white racial superiority.  
* ***Annexation***: The process of taking territory and adding it to a country's own possessions.  
* ***Blockade***: The use of naval power to prevent merchant ships reaching an enemy country, disrupting its economy and capacity to make war.  
* ***Liberalism***: The idea of personal and economic freedom, particularly the right to property, freedom of speech and worship, freedom to take part in politics and free trade.

## *Peace Without Victory*

Wilson had won a significant victory using diplomacy. This encouraged him to attempt to broker peace in Europe with the USA playing the leading role in creating a new international order. On 18 December 1916 he issued a '*Peace Note*' calling on both sides to clarify their war aims. Earlier in the month, in a move to put pressure on Britain and France, he instructed the Federal Bank to freeze loans to the two countries, reversing the policy of the previous two years.

On 22 January 1917, in a dramatic speech asking for '*peace without victory*', he called for a new international order based on a League of Nations, disarmament and freedom of the seas. In a major turning point in American foreign policy, the president was asserting leadership on a global scale.

Wilson's intervention was quickly wrecked by the German high command which, frustrated by its inability to achieve decisive victory in 1916, threw caution to the winds and announced the resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare in January 1917.

## *The Road to War*

This was an enormous risk for Germany: fully expecting the USA to enter the war, it gambled on breaking the Allies, particularly Britain, by economic blockade before the USA could intervene.

Wilson responded by breaking off diplomatic relations with Germany. The American public mood hardened as ships were sunk. The German campaign nearly succeeded and only the introduction of convoys saved Britain in the summer of 1917.

The final provocation for the USA came with the publication of the Zimmerman telegraph in March 1917. A message from the German foreign minister, Zimmerman, to the Mexican government suggested an alliance and exploited the raw feelings in Mexico after the US intervention in the revolution. There was a vague promise that Germany would assist Mexico to recover territories it had lost to the USA in 1846-48.

The message was intercepted by British intelligence and leaked to the US ambassador in London. The German message was seen, at the very least, as a violation of the Monroe Doctrine and, even more seriously, as an aggressive threat to US security.

On 2 April 1917 Wilson asked Congress for a declaration of war against Germany. Although the threat to American national interests prompted the declaration of war, Wilson was careful to emphasise America's moral superiority with the ringing claim, '*The world must be made safe for democracy*.' Overwhelmingly, Congress voted for war.

## *The American Contribution to Allied Victory*

The Americans brought fresh heart to an Allied cause that was taking a battering from the collapse of Russia, the submarine campaign and the deadlock on the Western Front. America's industrial and financial strength now sustained Germany's enemies with ample supplies of munitions and finance.

America's powerful navy joined the Royal Navy. The German submarine campaign was defeated with the introduction of convoys and American shipyards replaced most of the shipping lost. The Allied blockade inflicted severe hardship and hunger on Germany's home front, a significant factor in Germany's defeat.

The American army was rapidly built up after May 1917. By March 1918 there were 300,000 US troops in France and by November 1918 there were 2 million. Led by General Pershing, American forces operated independently. In the last major German attacks on the Western Front in May and June 1918, American troops played important roles at Chateau-Thierry and Belleau Wood in resisting the German advance.

The US army also played its part in the series of Allied offensives on the Western Front which broke German resistance in the autumn of 1918. The Meuse-Argonne offensive, planned and executed by Pershing, involved 1.2 million US soldiers. By the time Germany asked for an armistice in November 1918, the USA had suffered 109,000 fatal casualties — heavy losses for the comparatively short period of time the USA had been involved.

Although the Americans made many direct contributions to victory, their indirect effect in convincing Germany it could not win the war in the long term may have been decisive.

## *The Peace Treaties*

### *The Fourteen Points*

President Wilson had already made clear his agenda in his message of January 1917 before the US declaration of war. He followed this up in January 1918 with the publication of his **Fourteen Points**. He insisted that America was not at war with Germany as one of the Allies: the USA was an associated power. Without consulting any of the Allies, he announced his plans to Congress. The Fourteen Points had as their aim a new world order after the war based on *self-determination* for the peoples of the Austro-Hungarian empire, disarmament, freedom of the seas, an end to secret diplomacy and, crucially, the establishment of a League of Nations to keep the peace in the future. This was a bold attempt by an American president to dominate the post-war world: in its scope and ambition nothing like it had been seen before.

### *The Peace Negotiations*

When Germany sought an armistice in November 1918, it did so on the basis that it should be based on the Fourteen Points. The Allies, however, were intent on a more punitive peace, requiring *reparations* from their enemy and annexations of enemy territory. In the period before negotiations began, Wilson made a miscalculation — given that the Republicans now controlled both the House of Representatives and the Senate, he would have been wise to reach out for their support to make sure the eventual treaty would be *ratified*. However, he refused to engage with his political opponents and left for Europe determined to negotiate the peace treaty on his own.

Wilson did achieve some success in the peace treaty negotiations. The League of Nations was incorporated into the treaty. Self-determination was an important principle accepted for the most part by the peacemakers. However, Wilson had to compromise on the issue of reparations: harsh though the treaty was in this respect, it would probably have been harsher still without Wilson, and there certainly would not have been a League of Nations.

### *Opposition to Wilson's Policy*

The difficulty arose when he returned to the USA. Many American politicians were not happy with the concept of the League of Nations, partly because it could embroil the USA in further conflict in enforcing *collective security*. More significantly, critics led by the Republican Henry Cabot Lodge asserted that it undermined the US Constitution by limiting the power of Congress to declare war. Wilson would not compromise, nor would his Republican opponents.

Wilson suffered an incapacitating stroke on 2 October 1919 which ended his political career. The treaty failed to get the necessary two-thirds majority for ratification in the Senate. Eventually, an American peace treaty was signed with Germany but the USA never joined the League of Nations because of the Senate vote. The League of Nations was formed but had no success in preventing the aggression of Japan and Italy against league members in the 1930s. Wilsonian internationalism was at an end and the Republicans secured an enormous victory in the 1920 presidential election.

## *Themes in US Foreign Policy, 1890-1920*

### *Imperialism*

The period is sometimes identified as an era of American imperialism. While the annexation of new territories from the defeated Spanish and the expansion of American influence in Latin America and the Pacific provide evidence of the spread of American power, it is also true that many Americans were uneasy at this development. America's very foundation as an independent country in 1776 was in itself anti-imperialist, a tradition partly exemplified by the Monroe Doctrine.

### *Latin America*

The development of America's powerful economy was a major factor in the demand for the protection of overseas trade and bases. The penetration of Latin American countries by American business interests did influence the direction of American foreign policy. The Roosevelt Corollary, with the consequent intervention in Latin American countries, was applied consistently by Republican and Democrat presidents in the period 1890-1920.

### *Idealism*

Although a racist social Darwinist twist was subsequently given to the concept of Manifest Destiny, there was nevertheless a strong moral, even idealistic, streak in American foreign policy. As Abraham Lincoln put it in 1862 after signing the Emancipation Proclamation, the USA was the '*last best hope*' on earth for democracy. The strongest exponent of this idealism was probably Woodrow Wilson, who was quick to portray Germany as undemocratic, militaristic and ruled by an autocratic Kaiser. When the Germans asked for an armistice in October 1918, it was Wilson who insisted that Germany had to change its government and remove the Kaiser before the USA would accept an armistice.

### *National Interests*

It should not be forgotten that Wilson went to war to protect American freedom of the seas and because of the threat to national interests revealed in the Zimmerman telegram. Wilsonian internationalism did not necessarily exclude the protection of US national interests.

### *Economic Power*

By 1920 there was no doubt that America had emerged on the world stage as the dominant economic power, not only in terms of its industrial strength but also in its financial grip on the other leading powers in the world. Germany, Britain and France were now financially dependent on the USA.

### *Great-Power Status*

Diplomatically the USA had also come of age. While Wilson's Fourteen Points had not been adopted in their entirety, his imprint on the peace settlement and the creation of the League of Nations was indelible. By 1920 the USA also dominated the Caribbean and had become a Pacific power. Whereas it had been seen as a second-rate power in 1890, by 1920 the USA was economically dominant, and it had the second most powerful navy in the world (soon to be equal to the Royal Navy), a huge army of 2 million men which had contributed to German defeat, and a president who had asserted an unprecedented claim to world leadership both during and after the First World War.

## *How Far Was US Foreign Policy Dominated by Isolationism After 1920?*

Wilson's failure to achieve agreement on his concept of a League of Nations, a reaction against the cost of US involvement in the First World War and disapproval of the perceived selfish, imperialistic behaviour of its allies at Versailles all contributed to a strong isolationist trend in US foreign policy after the war.

### *Economic Nationalism*

The protection of the American economy was given a huge priority with the Fordney-McCumber Act of 1922, which introduced the highest import tariffs ever imposed by a US government. This narrow economic nationalism was reinforced by the onset of the Great Depression and the Hawley-Smoot Tariff Act of 1930 — a further attempt to protect the US economy which had a disastrous impact on world trade by deepening the Depression.

American public opinion was naturally more concerned with the domestic impact of the Great Depression rather than events abroad. Distrust of bankers and big business, who had the most to gain from international connections, was also a factor influencing the isolationist trend.

### *Nye Committee*

In 1934 the Senate appointed an isolationist Republican politician, Gerald Nye, to investigate the armaments trade. The Nye Committee's hearings provided plenty of evidence of the questionable practices of the munitions trade and the huge profits generated by American industrialists and financiers in the First World War. Many Americans drew the uncomfortable conclusion that US entry into the war in 1917 had been driven by special interest groups who stood to profit from the economic benefits of war. An opinion poll suggested that 70% of Americans felt that entry into the First World War had been a mistake.

### *Neutrality Acts*

Mindful of Wilson's promises in the 1916 election that he would keep America out of the European war and influenced by the findings of the Nye Committee, Congress passed important legislation to prevent a repeat of 1917. In 1935 the first Neutrality Act required the president to declare an arms embargo against all sides in a war and empowered him to warn American citizens not to travel on ships of the warring countries. The memory of the Lusitania and Mussolini's attack on Ethiopia in 1935 clearly motivated Congress.

The Second Neutrality Act in 1936 prohibited war loans and credits to any belligerent. The Third Neutrality Act 1937 confirmed the earlier provision and made travel on belligerent ships unlawful.

Nevertheless, it would be misleading to describe American policy in this period as exclusively isolationist.

### *Reparations*

The importance of the economy reflected in the high tariff policy of the period also meant that America was inextricably linked to international economic obligations. It had lent $10.35 billion to its wartime allies — adopting a high tariff policy meant that countries like Britain and France could not rely on increased manufacturing trade to pay off their debts. Both had also exhausted their gold reserves during the war. The only way forward for them was to rely on reparations payments from Germany to pay off their debt.

When Germany defaulted on reparations payments in 1923 during the occupation of the Ruhr, the USA was forced to intervene. It could not distance itself from European affairs as easily as the isolationists wanted. The Americans instituted the Dawes Plan in 1924 and the Young Plan in 1929, which rescheduled German reparation payments, and invested $2.5 billion in Germany so that US allies could repay their war debt. Although this stabilised the situation, the collapse of the American economy after 1929 completely undermined war debt repayments and most of the economies of Europe.

### *Washington Naval Conference*

Despite the Republican suspicion of the League of Nations, the Americans took the initiative in organising the Washington Naval Conference in 1921. This was a full-scale attempt to limit expenditure on armaments, particularly naval armaments. Disarmament was seen as a popular way of avoiding war and perhaps, more importantly, as a way of reducing government expenditure. There were concerns about Japan's increasingly powerful navy, its alliance with Britain (since 1902) and the potential threat to American interests in the Pacific and in China. The results of the conference were:

* A ten-year period during which no capital ships were to be built by any major power  
* The leading naval powers of the world were to accept a 5:5:3:1.75:1.75 ratio in capital ships for Britain, the USA, Japan, France and Italy  
* The end of the Anglo-Japanese treaty  
* Britain signed away its naval supremacy, accepting parity with the USA as it simply did not have the financial resources to compete

The conference was the first international agreement on arms limitation, significantly convened by the USA during a period of so-called isolationism.

### *Kellogg-Briand Pact, 1928*

In 1928 the American secretary of state, Frank Kellogg, signed an agreement renouncing war. Known as the **Kellogg-Briand Pact** (after Kellogg and the French foreign minister), it was supported by 62 countries. The agreement was only symbolic as there was no mechanism to enforce its provisions but, as with the Washington Naval Conference, it showed that American policy was not strictly isolationist.

### *Latin America*

American interest in Latin America remained a constant thread in its foreign policy. American investment and economic interests in Latin American countries were huge, estimated at $3.5 billion by 1929. However, the 1920s did see some relaxation of the American military presence: troops were withdrawn from Cuba in 1922 and Santo Domingo in 1924. President Hoover (1929-33) made a goodwill tour of 11 Latin American countries in 1930 and significantly did not intervene when revolutions broke out in Brazil, Cuba and Panama in 1930-31. President Roosevelt (1933-45) announced a '*Good Neighbour*' policy in Latin America. One of its most significant results was Roosevelt's acceptance of Mexico's decision in 1938 to nationalise all foreign oil companies — there was no US military intervention despite the powerful lobbying of the US Standard Oil Company.

Despite the strength of isolationism, the deteriorating situation in Europe and Asia would prompt a reappraisal of US foreign policy.
