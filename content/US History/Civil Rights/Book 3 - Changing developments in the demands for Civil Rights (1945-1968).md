
# 1. Supreme Court Activism in the 1950s

## Background
- The Supreme Court became a crucial ally for civil rights activists in the 1950s
- Led by Chief Justice Earl Warren (appointed 1953), the Court took a more liberal stance on civil rights issues
- This represented a significant shift from earlier Courts that had upheld segregation

## Key Factors for Judicial Activism
- **NAACP Legal Strategy**: The NAACP's persistent litigation, led by brilliant lawyers like Thurgood Marshall
- **Cold War Context**: The US could hardly claim leadership of the "free world" while maintaining racial discrimination at home
- **Truman Administration Support**: President Truman's briefs to the Court argued against segregation
- **Warren's Leadership**: Warren worked to achieve unanimous decisions to give rulings greater moral authority

# 2. Brown vs Topeka Board of Education (1954)

## Background
- Oliver Brown challenged the segregation of Topeka, Kansas schools where his daughter Linda had to travel 21 blocks to a segregated black school despite a white school being closer
- The NAACP supported Brown as part of its strategy to challenge the "separate but equal" doctrine established in Plessy v. Ferguson (1896)

## The Case
- **Arguments**: Thurgood Marshall argued that segregation violated the 14th Amendment's Equal Protection Clause
- **Psychological Evidence**: The NAACP presented research showing segregation created feelings of inferiority in black children
- **Supreme Court Ruling (1954)**: Unanimous decision that "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal"

## Significance
- **Legal Impact**: Overturned Plessy v. Ferguson, removing constitutional basis for segregation
- **Moral Impact**: Established that segregation itself was harmful, not just unequal facilities
- **Limitations**: No timeline for implementation was set, leading to resistance and delay

## Resistance to Desegregation
- **Southern Manifesto (1956)**: Signed by 101 southern congressmen opposing Brown
- **White Citizens' Councils**: Formed throughout the South to resist desegregation (250,000 members by 1956)
- **Massive Resistance**: Some states closed public schools rather than integrate
- **Little Rock Crisis (1957)**:
  - Governor Orval Faubus used National Guard to prevent nine black students from entering Central High School
  - President Eisenhower eventually sent federal troops to enforce desegregation
  - Demonstrated that Supreme Court rulings required federal enforcement to be effective

# 3. Peaceful Protests

## Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955-1956)

### Background
- Montgomery, Alabama enforced strict Jim Crow laws on buses
- Black passengers had to sit at the back and give up seats to white passengers
- Rosa Parks, NAACP activist, refused to give up her seat on 1 December 1955

### The Boycott
- **Organisation**: Led by Montgomery Improvement Association (MIA) with Martin Luther King Jr. as president
- **Participation**: 50,000 black residents boycotted buses for 381 days
- **Methods**: Car pools, walking, community organisation through black churches
- **Challenges**: King's home was bombed; he was arrested; KKK intimidation

### Outcome
- **Supreme Court Ruling (November 1956)**: Browder v. Gayle declared segregation on public buses unconstitutional
- **Success**: Buses were desegregated in Montgomery
- **Wider Impact**:
  - Established King as national civil rights leader
  - Led to formation of Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC) in 1957
  - Demonstrated effectiveness of non-violent mass protest and economic boycotts

## Student Sit-ins (1960)

### Origins
- Began in Greensboro, North Carolina when four black college students sat at a segregated Woolworth's lunch counter
- Sparked similar protests across the South involving 70,000 students

### Impact
- Led to desegregation of lunch counters in 150 southern cities
- Resulted in formation of Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC)
- Demonstrated power of direct action by younger generation of activists

## Freedom Rides (1961)

### Purpose
- Organised by CORE to test compliance with Supreme Court rulings banning segregation on interstate transportation
- Mixed groups of black and white activists rode buses through the South

### Events
- **Violence in Alabama**: Riders attacked in Anniston and Birmingham; police commissioner "Bull" Connor allowed violence to occur
- **Federal Response**: Attorney General Robert Kennedy eventually enforced desegregation of interstate travel facilities

### Significance
- Demonstrated brutality of segregation to national audience
- Forced Kennedy administration to take stronger stance on civil rights
- Showed limitations of federal enforcement without presidential commitment

# 4. Martin Luther King Jr. & Assassination (1968)

## King's Philosophy
- **Nonviolent Resistance**: Inspired by Christian teachings and Gandhi
- **Civil Disobedience**: Willingness to break unjust laws and accept punishment
- **Beloved Community**: Vision of integrated society based on love and justice

## Major Campaigns

### Birmingham Campaign (1963)
- **Target**: Most segregated city in the South
- **Tactics**: Mass marches, sit-ins, boycotts
- **Response**: Police used dogs and water cannons on protesters (including children)
- **Outcome**: 
  - "Letter from Birmingham Jail" - King's defense of nonviolent protest
  - Led to desegregation agreement in Birmingham
  - Prompted Kennedy to propose Civil Rights Act

### March on Washington (1963)
- **Organisers**: A. Philip Randolph, Bayard Rustin, and other civil rights leaders
- **Attendance**: 250,000 people (including 60,000 white participants)
- **King's Speech**: "I Have a Dream" became defining moment of movement
- **Impact**: Increased national support for civil rights legislation

### Selma Campaign (1965)
- **Goal**: Voting rights for African Americans
- **Violence**: "Bloody Sunday" - state troopers attacked peaceful marchers on Edmund Pettus Bridge
- **Result**: Led to Voting Rights Act of 1965

### Later Years and Assassination
- **Chicago Campaign (1966)**: Attempted to address de facto segregation and economic inequality in North
- **Opposition to Vietnam War**: Alienated some supporters but reflected broader social justice vision
- **Assassination**: 4 April 1968 in Memphis, Tennessee while supporting sanitation workers' strike
- **Legacy**: 
  - Civil Rights Act of 1968 (Fair Housing Act) passed shortly after
  - Established as national hero and symbol of nonviolent struggle

# 5. Presidential Leadership: Eisenhower, Kennedy & Johnson

## Dwight D. Eisenhower (1953-1961)
- **Initial Reluctance**: Did not strongly support civil rights, believed change couldn't be legislated
- **Key Actions**:
  - Sent federal troops to Little Rock (1957) to enforce desegregation
  - Signed Civil Rights Acts of 1957 and 1960 (weak but first federal civil rights legislation since Reconstruction)
- **Limitations**: No strong moral leadership on civil rights issues

## John F. Kennedy (1961-1963)
- **Initial Approach**: Cautious, needed southern Democratic support
- **Evolution**:
  - Initially slow to act on civil rights
  - Birmingham protests forced his hand
  - June 1963 speech: "We are confronted primarily with a moral issue"
  - Proposed Civil Rights Act
- **Key Actions**:
  - Sent federal marshals to protect James Meredith at University of Mississippi (1962)
  - Brother Robert Kennedy as Attorney General took stronger stance
- **Assassination (November 1963)**: Created momentum for civil rights legislation as memorial

## Lyndon B. Johnson (1963-1969)
- **Background**: Southern Democrat with legislative experience
- **Key Achievements**:
  - **Civil Rights Act (1964)**:
    - Prohibited discrimination in public accommodations
    - Authorised federal government to desegregate public facilities
    - Created Equal Employment Opportunity Commission
    - Banned discrimination in programs receiving federal funds
  - **Voting Rights Act (1965)**:
    - Suspended literacy tests
    - Authorised federal examiners to register voters
    - Dramatically increased black voter registration
- **Motivation**: Combination of moral conviction and political calculation
- **Limitations**: Vietnam War diverted resources and attention from "Great Society" programs

# 6. Black Power in the North

## Origins
- **Frustration with Slow Progress**: Especially in northern ghettos where de facto segregation persisted
- **Influence of Malcolm X**: Promoted black self-determination and criticised nonviolence
- **Urban Unrest**: Watts riot (1965) and others highlighted economic inequality

## Key Figures and Organisations 

### Malcolm X (1925-1965)
- **Early Life**: Criminal career, conversion to Nation of Islam in prison
- **Philosophy**: 
  - Black separatism
  - Self-defense "by any means necessary"
  - International perspective on racial struggle
- **Evolution**: Left Nation of Islam (1964), adopted more inclusive view after pilgrimage to Mecca
- **Assassination**: 21 February 1965 by Nation of Islam members

### Stokely Carmichael and SNCC
- **Meredith March (1966)**: Carmichael popularised "Black Power" slogan
- **SNCC Transformation**: From interracial organisation to black separatism
- **Expulsion of White Members**: Symbolised shift in movement philosophy

### Black Panther Party (Founded 1966)
- **Leaders**: Huey Newton and Bobby Seale
- **Ten-Point Program**: Called for full employment, housing, education, and self-determination
- **Methods**: 
  - Armed patrols to monitor police brutality
  - Community service programs (free breakfast for children, health clinics)
  - Political education
- **Government Response**: FBI targeted Panthers under COINTELPRO program

## Black Power Philosophy
- **Core Principles**:
  - Self-determination for black communities
  - Racial pride and cultural heritage
  - Economic self-sufficiency
  - Political power through organisation
- **Variations**: From cultural nationalism to revolutionary socialism
- **Impact**: 
  - Shifted focus from integration to black empowerment
  - Inspired cultural expressions (Afro hairstyles, dashikis)
  - Created institutions (black studies programs, businesses)

# 7. Urban Riots (1965-1968)

## Major Riots

### Watts Riot (Los Angeles, August 1965)
- **Trigger**: Arrest of black motorist by white police officer
- **Duration**: 6 days
- **Impact**: 34 deaths, 1,000+ injuries, $40 million property damage
- **Significance**: First major northern urban riot, occurred just days after Voting Rights Act

### Newark and Detroit Riots (1967)
- **Newark**: 26 deaths, occurred over 5 days
- **Detroit**: 43 deaths, worst riot of decade
- **Pattern**: Police violence as trigger, underlying economic grievances

### Kerner Commission (1968)
- **Establishment**: President Johnson appointed commission to investigate urban riots
- **Findings**:
  - Primary cause was white racism
  - Identified deep racial inequality in housing, education, employment
  - Warning: "Our nation is moving toward two societies, one black, one white—separate and unequal"
- **Recommendations**: Massive federal investment in urban communities
- **Response**: Limited implementation due to Vietnam War and white backlash

### Causes of Urban Unrest
- **Economic Factors**: High unemployment, poor housing, inadequate schools
- **Political Factors**: Lack of representation, police brutality
- **Psychological Factors**: Frustration with slow pace of change
- **International Context**: Rising expectations amid global decolonisation

# 8. Assessment and Legacy

## Achievements of Civil Rights Movement (1954-1968)
- **Legal End to Segregation**: Civil Rights Act (1964) and Voting Rights Act (1965)
- **Increased Political Participation**: Dramatic rise in black voter registration and officials
- **Shift in Public Opinion**: Changed attitudes toward racial equality
- **Inspiration for Other Movements**: Women's rights, anti-war, environmental movements

## Limitations
- **Economic Inequality**: Remained largely unaddressed
- **De Facto Segregation**: Continued in northern cities
- **White Backlash**: Contributed to political realignment (southern strategy)
- **Movement Fragmentation**: Divisions between integrationists and separatists

### Historiographical Debates
- **Top-down vs. Bottom-up**: Relative importance of national leaders vs. local activists
- **Nonviolence vs. Self-Defense**: Effectiveness of different strategies
- **Federal Government Role**: Ally or obstacle to progress?
- **Long-term Impact**: How much fundamental change was achieved?

## Key Terms

- **De Jure Segregation**: Legal segregation enforced by laws (primarily Southern)
- **De Facto Segregation**: Segregation resulting from practice and custom (primarily Northern)
- **Nonviolent Direct Action**: Peaceful protests that deliberately break unjust laws
- **Civil Disobedience**: Public refusal to comply with laws as a form of protest
- **States' Rights**: Doctrine that states should resist federal intervention in their affairs
- **Black Power**: Movement emphasising racial pride, self-determination, and equality
- **Filibuster**: Prolonged speech to prevent vote on legislation (used by southern senators to block civil rights bills)

## Timeline of Key Events

- **1954**: Brown v. Board of Education decision
- **1955-56**: Montgomery Bus Boycott
- **1957**: Little Rock Crisis; Civil Rights Act
- **1960**: Founding of SNCC; Sit-in movement
- **1961**: Freedom Rides
- **1963**: Birmingham Campaign; March on Washington; Kennedy assassinated
- **1964**: Civil Rights Act; Freedom Summer
- **1965**: Selma Campaign; Voting Rights Act; Watts Riot
- **1966**: Meredith March; "Black Power" slogan popularised 
- **1967**: Urban riots in Newark and Detroit
- **1968**: King assassinated; Civil Rights Act (Fair Housing)
