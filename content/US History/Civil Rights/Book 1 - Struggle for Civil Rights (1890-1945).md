# Introduction

At the end of the American Civil War in 1865, slavery was abolished by the 13th Amendment to the Constitution. Four million Black Americans were legally free, but it was unclear whether they had the same civil rights as white Americans. The Reconstruction period (1865-1877) aimed to rebuild the South and integrate freed slaves into American society.

## Key Terms

- **Constitution**: The rules and procedures by which the USA is governed, which can be amended by Congress.
- **Democratic Party**: Political party that had been pro-slavery and opposed civil rights for Black Americans.
- **Republican Party**: Political party founded in the 1850s that opposed slavery and whose radical members campaigned for civil rights.
- **Ku Klux Klan (KKK)**: Terrorist organisation that used violence and intimidation to uphold white supremacy.
- **Segregated Society**: Where people are separated by race in education, housing, and transportation.
- **Civil Rights**: The right to vote in elections, to equal treatment under the law, and to equal opportunities in education and the workplace.
- **Freedmen**: People freed from slavery.

# Disenfranchisement

Disenfranchisement is the process of taking away the right to vote. After Reconstruction, Black American voters in the South were systematically disenfranchised through various legal and extralegal means.

## Methods of Disenfranchisement

1. **Poll Tax**: A tax imposed on voters, making it difficult for poorer Black Americans to vote.
   - Rules for payment were deliberately complex and expensive.
   - Georgia introduced a poll tax as early as 1871.

2. **Literacy Tests**: Tests designed to be extremely difficult for Black Americans to pass.
   - Required detailed knowledge of the US Constitution.
   - White registrars manipulated these tests to disqualify literate Black Americans.

3. **Grandfather Clauses**: Laws allowing men to vote if their father or grandfather had voted before 1867.
   - Since no Black Americans could vote before 1867, these clauses effectively excluded them while allowing illiterate whites to vote.

4. **Understanding Clauses**: Allowed registrars to enrol illiterate men if they could demonstrate understanding of a section of the state constitution when read to them.
   - Easily abused to allow whites to vote while denying Black Americans the same right.

5. **White Primaries**: In the one-party South, Democrats introduced whites-only primary elections.
   - Since winning the Democratic primary was equivalent to winning the election, this effectively excluded Black Americans from the political process.

6. **Violence and Intimidation**: The KKK and similar groups used terror to prevent Black Americans from voting.

## Impact of Disenfranchisement

- By 1910, the Black vote had been virtually eliminated in southern states.
- Registration rates dropped dramatically: in Arkansas, 71% of the Black electorate voted in 1890, but only 9% voted after a poll tax was introduced.
- By 1900, only 3% of Black Southern males could vote.
- Disenfranchisement also affected poor white voters, though grandfather clauses were created to mitigate this impact.
- The South was far less democratic in 1900 than it had been in 1860.

# Impact of Supreme Court Decisions

The Supreme Court is the highest court in the USA. Its rulings are final and ensure that neither the president nor Congress abuses the powers given by the Constitution. Several Supreme Court decisions undermined the civil rights guaranteed to Black Americans by the post-Civil War amendments.

## Key Supreme Court Cases

1. **Civil Rights Cases (1883)**:
   - The Court examined five cases where Black Americans had sued transportation companies, hotels, and theatres for racial discrimination.
   - Ruled that the Civil Rights Act of 1875 was unconstitutional.
   - Declared that the federal government had no authority to protect Black Americans against discrimination by private individuals.
   - Justice John Marshall Harlan dissented, claiming the ruling would contribute to the loss of Black civil rights.

2. **Plessy v. Ferguson (1896)**:
   - Homer Plessy, a Black American, challenged Louisiana's law requiring separate accommodation for different races on trains.
   - The Court ruled that segregation was legal as long as facilities were "separate but equal."
   - This decision gave constitutional approval to Jim Crow laws.
   - Justice Harlan famously dissented, stating "our Constitution is colour-blind."

3. **Cumming v. Board of Education (1899)**:
   - Extended the "separate but equal" principle to schools.
   - The Court rejected a challenge to a Georgia county that continued funding a white high school while closing a Black one.
   - This ruling approved segregated schools across the South.

4. **Williams v. Mississippi (1898)**:
   - Upheld Mississippi's constitution requiring voters to pass literacy tests and pay a poll tax.
   - The Court ruled these requirements were not discriminatory, despite their clear impact on Black voting rights.
   - This decision effectively nullified the 15th Amendment's protections.

## Impact of Supreme Court Decisions

- These rulings reflected and reinforced the prevailing white supremacist attitudes of the time.
- They provided legal justification for segregation and disenfranchisement.
- The Court's interpretations of the Reconstruction Amendments were narrow and limited their effectiveness.
- By endorsing "separate but equal," the Court gave legitimacy to a system that was separate but rarely equal.

# Jim Crow Laws

Jim Crow laws were state and local laws that enforced racial segregation in the Southern United States. Named after a racist minstrel show character, these laws were more far-reaching, detailed, and strictly enforced than anything that had existed before.

## Origins and Spread

- The term "Jim Crow" originated from a character in minstrel shows performed by white actor Thomas Rice in the 1830s.
- Florida passed the first Jim Crow law in 1887, requiring separate accommodation for races on trains.
- By 1891, most southern states had passed similar laws.
- After the Plessy decision in 1896, Jim Crow laws spread to almost every aspect of public life.

## Scope of Jim Crow Laws

Jim Crow laws mandated segregation in:
- Transportation (trains, streetcars, stations)
- Public facilities (theatres, parks, restaurants)
- Education (schools, universities)
- Housing (residential districts)
- Healthcare (hospitals)
- Even cemeteries

The laws were extremely detailed, regulating:
- That textbooks for white and Black schools could not be stored together
- That Black and white people were forbidden to play checkers together
- That whites were not to use Black prostitutes

## Impact of Jim Crow Laws

- Created a daily reminder of Black inequality.
- Established a legal framework for white supremacy.
- Made racial segregation a normal, accepted part of Southern life.
- Extended beyond the South, influencing race relations in Northern cities as well.

# Racial Violence

By 1900, Black Americans were largely denied the vote and more rigidly segregated than ever before. The idea of white supremacy encouraged a climate of racial violence.

## Lynching

Lynching reached its peak in the 1890s:
- In 1892 alone, 235 Black Americans were lynched by white mobs.
- Between 1882 and 1930, there were 2,805 victims, predominantly Black Americans, in ten southern states.
- Lynching was often aided by the collusion of law enforcement officials.
- Scenes of extreme cruelty were common, such as the case of Sam Hose in 1899, who was mutilated and burned alive before 2,000 onlookers.

### Justifications for Lynching

- Southern defenders often portrayed lynching as a defence against Black sexual assaults on white women.
- However, most lynching was provoked by other alleged crimes and economic competition.
- 50 of the victims were Black women, some of them pregnant.
- There were no convictions for participation in lynching until 1918.

### Impact of Lynching

- Created a climate of fear that deterred any challenge to white-dominated society.
- Served as a powerful tool of racial control.
- Reinforced the idea that Black Americans were outside the protection of the law.

## Race Riots

- Mob attacks on Black residential areas occurred with savage frequency.
- In 1898 in Wilmington, North Carolina, 11 Black Americans were killed and hundreds made homeless by a white mob.
- Similar attacks occurred in Atlanta and New Orleans in 1900.
- Race riots were not exclusively a Southern phenomenon, plaguing Northern cities such as New York, Philadelphia, and Chicago.

# Conditions for Black Americans in 1900 (PESCL)

## Political Conditions

- Nearly complete disenfranchisement in the South.
- No Black representation in Congress or state legislatures by 1915.
- Disenfranchisement prevented Black jury service, ensuring no legal equality.
- The federal government showed little interest in enforcing the 14th and 15th Amendments.

## Economic Conditions

- Around 85% of Black Americans lived in southern states where the per capita income was around half the national average.
- By 1910, 20% of Black farmers owned their own land.
- Significant increase in Black-owned businesses between 1880 and 1900.
- Businesses catering exclusively to Black customers did particularly well in a segregated society.
- Black insurance companies and banks prospered as white-owned companies discriminated against Black customers.

## Social Conditions

- Rigid segregation in all aspects of life.
- Pervasive fear of violence and intimidation.
- Limited social interaction between races.
- Black communities developed their own institutions and social networks.

## Cultural Conditions

- Growth of Black universities, often funded by religious missionary societies and aided by land grants.
- By 1900, 1.5 million Black Americans were attending school.
- The Peabody Fund and the Julius Rosenwald Fund supported teacher training.
- Huge discrepancy between expenditure on schools for Black Americans and those for whites.

## Legal Conditions

- Supreme Court decisions had undermined constitutional protections.
- "Separate but equal" doctrine established by Plessy v. Ferguson.
- Legal system offered little protection to Black Americans.
- All-white juries rarely convicted whites for crimes against Black Americans.

# Why Racial Segregation and White Supremacy Became Entrenched After 1890

## The Republican Party's Waning Support

- The Republican Party had traditionally been the main supporter of civil rights for Black Americans.
- After Reconstruction, Black votes became less important to the Republican Party as it gained more votes in Northern states.
- Northern Republicans grew increasingly unsympathetic toward Black Republicans.

## Fading Civil War Memories

- Memories of the Civil War faded, particularly after 1898 when a new American nationalism formed during the Spanish-American War.
- Northern and Southern whites fought together while Black Americans were mostly relegated to segregated units.
- White racial superiority became identified with ideas of nationalism during the war and the acquisition of new territories.

## Supreme Court Rulings

- Supreme Court decisions undermined civil rights to such an extent that few Black Americans retained the right to vote by the early twentieth century.
- These rulings generally reflected public opinion and were plausible interpretations of the Constitution.

## Federal Government Inaction

- The federal government lacked the political will to enforce the 14th and 15th Amendments and the Civil Rights Act of 1875.
- Politics was preoccupied with other issues, notably arguments about tariffs, trusts, and silver.
- A Democrat-dominated Congress repealed most of the enforcement legislation passed during Reconstruction.

## Fear of Black Advancement

- There was fear that a new generation of Black Americans presented a threat to the established order.
- Segregation was seen as a solution to the problems created by a post-slavery society.
- Pseudo-scientific race theories emerged, such as Charles Carroll's "The Negro a Beast" (1900), which placed Black Americans nearer to apes than to human beings.

## Constitutional Powers of States

- The Constitution gave Southern states power over voting, education, transport, and law enforcement.
- These powers enabled segregation to spread and continue.

# Responses to the Erosion of Civil Rights

## Booker T. Washington (1856-1915)

### Background and Philosophy

- Born into slavery in Virginia.
- Principal of Tuskegee Institute in Alabama.
- Advocated concentration on economic progress rather than immediate social and political equality.
- Believed in vocational education with emphasis on practical skills.
- His approach has been labelled "accommodationist" and was criticised by some Black Americans as being too passive.

### The Atlanta Compromise (1895)

- Washington's most famous speech at the opening of the Atlanta World Fair.
- Declared: "In all things that are purely social we can be as separate as the fingers, yet one as the hand in all things essential to mutual progress."
- Urged Black Americans to accept segregation for the time being and focus on economic advancement.
- Believed that economic progress would eventually lead to social and political equality.

### Influence and Achievements

- Regularly consulted by presidents and other politicians.
- Acknowledged as a leading spokesman for Black Americans.
- In 1901, President Theodore Roosevelt invited him to dine at the White House, causing controversy.
- Founded the National Negro Business League in 1900 to support Black enterprises.
- His autobiography "Up From Slavery" (1901) was widely read and influential.

### Criticisms

- Critics argued that acceptance of white supremacy would never bring about change.
- W.E.B. Du Bois claimed Washington's approach made Southern whites even worse to Black Americans.
- His dismissal of voting rights drew particular criticism.
- Some Black leaders felt he was too willing to compromise with white supremacists.

### Assessment

- Washington's private papers reveal that while he gave whites the impression he favoured segregation, he secretly financed and directed several court suits against segregation.
- Given the degree of white hostility, accommodationism may have been the only practical approach.
- He demonstrated that a Black born in slavery could become a nationally and internationally respected figure.
- He helped many individual Black Americans through the Tuskegee Institute.

## W.E.B. Du Bois (1868-1963)

### Background and Philosophy

- Born free in Massachusetts.
- First Black American to achieve a doctorate at Harvard.
- Professor at Atlanta University from 1896 to 1910.
- Attacked Washington's idea that economic gains were more important than civil rights.
- Argued that Black Americans should campaign vigorously for political, civil, and social rights.
- Advocated for a "Talented Tenth" of well-educated Black leaders to lead the fight for equality.

### The Niagara Movement (1905)

- Founded by Du Bois and William Monroe Trotter.
- Met on the Canadian side of Niagara Falls because no US hotel would accept them.
- Demanded voting rights and an end to all discrimination.
- Rejected Washington's accommodationism in favour of protest.
- Never gained mass support due to its academic approach and lack of funding.

### Role in the NAACP

- Played a leading role in establishing the NAACP in 1909.
- Served as director of research and publicity.
- Edited the NAACP's magazine "The Crisis" for 25 years.
- Used "The Crisis" to publicise racial injustices and advocate for change.

### Impact and Legacy

- Helped establish the most important twentieth-century Black organisation.
- His publications and assertions that Black Americans were a chosen people with special cultural and spiritual strengths contributed to increased Black pride.
- His intellectual élitism and frequent changes of mind help explain why he has been called a "leader without followers."
- Had less influence than Booker T. Washington in early twentieth-century America.

## Ida B. Wells (1862-1931)

### Background and Activism

- Born into slavery in Mississippi.
- Began activism in 1884 when she refused to surrender her train seat to a white man.
- Successfully sued the railroad company, though the decision was later overturned.
- Co-owned the Memphis Free Speech newspaper.
- Forced to flee Memphis in 1892 after publishing anti-lynching articles.

### Anti-Lynching Campaign

- Challenged the myth that lynching was primarily a response to rape of white women.
- Argued that lynching was often motivated by economic competition and racial control.
- Described the horrors of lynching in graphic detail to raise awareness.
- Wrote "Southern Horrors" (1893), a pamphlet exposing lynching.
- Wrote a letter to President McKinley in 1898 protesting the failure to take action against lynching.

### Organisational Work

- Helped found the NAACP in 1909.
- Established Chicago's first Black women's suffrage club in 1913.
- Worked for women's rights throughout her career.

### Impact and Legacy

- Put the issue of lynching in the public eye.
- Her work has been called "the beginning of the fight against white supremacy."
- For years after her death, she was virtually forgotten outside Chicago.
- Later Black militants and feminists revived interest in her life and career.

## The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP)

### Founding and Purpose

- Established in 1909 in response to the Springfield Riot of 1908.
- Aimed to make America's Black population "intellectually, politically, and socially free and equal."
- Founded by both Black and white activists.
- Du Bois played a leading role in its establishment.

### Approach and Strategy

- Investigated racism and publicised it.
- Proposed solutions to racial problems.
- Pursued legal action to enforce constitutional rights.
- Adopted a constitutional strategy, arguing that segregation and disenfranchisement violated the Reconstruction Amendments.

### Early Achievements

- In 1915, the Supreme Court ruled in Guinn v. United States that grandfather clauses were unconstitutional.
- Inspired the creation of the National Urban League in 1911, which focused on welfare and job/housing discrimination in Northern cities.
- Through "The Crisis," raised awareness of Black struggles and advanced arguments for change.

### Significance

- Became the most important Black civil rights organisation of the twentieth century.
- Demonstrated the possibility of interracial cooperation in the fight for civil rights.
- Established a legal strategy that would eventually lead to the dismantling of Jim Crow.

# Conclusion: The Erosion of Black Civil Rights (1877-1915)

## Losses

- The political influence Black Americans gained during Reconstruction vanished.
- Voting rights were systematically stripped in the South through state laws upheld by the courts.
- Disenfranchisement prevented Black jury service, ensuring no legal equality.
- Lynching and violence created a pervasive climate of fear.
- Segregation laws formalised and expanded racial separation.

## Limited Gains

- Black Americans retained the freedom to leave the South, and migration North increased.
- Washington's "Tuskegee machine" improved economic opportunities.
- A civil rights movement emerged with the founding of the NAACP.
- By 1915, foundations were laid for future Black advancement.

As historian Adam Fairclough noted: "By 1900 the southern Black population was more powerless than at any other time since the death of slavery." However, the resistance movements led by figures like Washington, Du Bois, Wells, and organisations like the NAACP laid the groundwork for the civil rights struggles of the twentieth century.
