# The Great Migration (1910s-1940s)

## Causes of Migration
- **Push Factors from the South:**
  - Jim Crow segregation and disenfranchisement
  - Widespread racial violence and lynching
  - Economic hardship: sharecropping poverty and boll weevil infestation
  - Limited educational and economic opportunities
- **Pull Factors to the North:**
  - Labor shortages due to WWI and reduced European immigration
  - Reports of better wages, voting rights, and improved conditions
  - Industrial expansion in northern cities

## Impact of WWI on Black Americans
- **Military Service:**
  - Over 350,000 served; only 40,000 saw combat
  - Segregated units (e.g., 369th "Harlem Hellfighters" regiment)
  - Limited combat roles; most assigned to labor units
  - Racist policies: Navy only accepted Black Americans as messmen
- **Post-War Tensions:**
  - Competition for jobs and housing led to race riots
  - 1919 Chicago riot: 23 Black and 15 white deaths after Black swimmer killed
  - "Red Summer" of racial violence across northern cities

## Experiences in the North
- **Racial Hostility:**
  - Formation of ghettos (Harlem, Chicago's South Side)
  - De facto segregation in housing, education, and public spaces
  - By 1930: 82% of Chicago's Black students attended all-Black schools
- **Limited Opportunities:**
  - Job concentration in manual labor and domestic service
  - Exclusion from unions and white-collar professions
  - Ford Motor Company employed 10,000 Black workers by 1926
  - Some government jobs: 3,400 in Chicago by 1928

## Resistance and Activism
- **NAACP Growth:**
  - Membership increased tenfold (1916-1919): 9,000 to 90,000
  - 300 branches by 1919
  - Legal challenges against segregation and lynching
- **Marcus Garvey and UNIA:**
  - Universal Negro Improvement Association (founded 1914)
  - Advocated racial pride, separatism, and return to Africa
  - Black Star Line shipping company
  - Claimed 4 million members at peak
  - Legacy influenced later Black Power movement
- **Political Progress:**
  - Oscar De Priest: first Black US Congressman of 20th century (1928)
  - A. Philip Randolph: Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters (1925)

## Cultural Impact
- **Harlem Renaissance:**
  - Flourishing of Black arts, literature, and music
  - Jazz musicians: Louis Armstrong, Duke Ellington, Fats Waller
  - Cultural stereotypes persisted (e.g., "Amos 'n' Andy" radio show)

# The Great Depression and New Deal (1929-1939)

## Impact of the Great Depression
- **Economic Devastation:**
  - Black unemployment twice that of white Americans
  - Collapse of cotton industry in the South
  - Poverty affected Black middle class businesses
- **Increased Discrimination:**
  - Employers gave preference to white workers
  - Reversal of WWI-era gains

## The New Deal: Mixed Impact
| **Negative Aspects** | **Positive Aspects** |
|----------------------|----------------------|
| Agricultural Adjustment Act led to eviction of 200,000 Black sharecroppers | 30% of Black families received relief by 1935 |
| Local committees often unsympathetic to Black farmers | WPA provided jobs for ~1 million Black families |
| NRA permitted unequal pay | ~1/3 of federally funded housing went to Black families |
| CCC had segregated camps | PWA invested in Black schools and hospitals (4x previous spending) |
| TVA used segregated accommodation | |

## Key Developments
- **Boycott Movement:**
  - Began in Chicago (1929) against discriminatory stores
  - Spread to 35 cities during 1930s
- **NAACP Strategy:**
  - Focus shifted to legal challenges against segregation
  - Walter White became Secretary (1931)

# World War II and Early Civil Rights (1941-1948)

## Home Front Experience
- **Defense Migration:**
  - Massive movement to industrial centers for war jobs
  - Racial tensions in workplaces (e.g., Alabama Dry Dock Company strike)
- **Urban Riots:**
  - **Detroit Riot (1943):** 34 deaths (25 Black), 800 injured, $2M damage
  - **Harlem Riot (1943):** Triggered by police shooting of Black soldier
- **Increased Activism:**
  - NAACP membership grew from 30,000 to 430,000
  - "Double V Campaign": Victory abroad against fascism, victory at home against racism

## Military Experience
- **Segregation Persists:**
  - Separate units, facilities, and even blood banks
  - Racial violence at 9 military training camps (1943)
- **Limited Progress:**
  - Tuskegee Airmen: segregated fighter unit with distinguished record
  - Temporary integration during Battle of the Bulge (1944)
  - By 1944: 701,678 Black Americans in army; 165,000 in navy
- **GI Bill of Rights (1944):**
  - Provided education, housing, and healthcare benefits
  - Offered path to economic advancement for veterans

## Federal Response
- **Fair Employment Practices Committee (FEPC):**
  - Established 1941 after A. Philip Randolph's threatened march
  - Promoted equality in defense industries
  - Limited effectiveness but set important precedent
- **Truman's Civil Rights Initiatives:**
  - President's Committee on Civil Rights (1946)
  - Executive Order 9980: ended discrimination in federal employment
  - Executive Order 9981: desegregated armed forces
  - Cost Truman support of Southern Democrats ("Dixiecrats")

## Legal Developments
- **Smith v. Allwright (1944):**
  - Supreme Court declared white primaries unconstitutional
  - Increased Black voter registration in South (3% to 12% by 1947)
- **Shelley v. Kraemer (1948):**
  - Supreme Court ruled racially restrictive covenants unenforceable

# Post-War Urban Changes

## Growth of Suburbs
- **Factors:**
  - Federal housing assistance (FHA, VA loans)
  - Increased car ownership and highway construction
  - White desire to escape urban problems
- **De Facto Segregation:**
  - Levittowns: racially exclusive housing developments
  - Restrictive covenants and white violence maintained segregation
  - "Redlining" by FHA reinforced residential segregation

## Changing Cities
- **Expanding Black Ghettos:**
  - White flight to suburbs
  - Discriminatory housing policies
  - Economic concentration in urban centers
- **Increased Racial Tensions:**
  - Competition for housing and jobs
  - Violent resistance to Black families in white neighborhoods

# Key Terms Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Communism** | Political belief in state control and planning of society |
| **Ghetto** | Urban area predominantly inhabited by members of the same racial or ethnic group |
| **Racial separatism** | The idea that African Americans should live separately from – but equal to – whites in all-black communities |
| **Boycott** | To ignore or isolate an organisation, usually to put pressure on companies by reducing their income from sales |
| **De jure segregation** | Racial segregation enforced by law |
| **De facto segregation** | Racial segregation that occurs in fact without legal sanction |
| **Redlining** | Discriminatory practice of denying services to residents of certain areas based on race or ethnicity |
| **Double V Campaign** | Campaign for victory against fascism abroad and against racism at home during WWII |
| **GI Bill of Rights** | Law passed in 1944 guaranteeing veterans loans for education, housing, and healthcare |
| **Dixiecrats** | Southern Democrats opposed to desegregation |

# Summary: Key Developments (1910-1948)

| Period | Key Developments | Impact on Black Americans |
|--------|------------------|---------------------------|
| **Great Migration** | Movement from South to North; WWI labor shortages | Created urban ghettos; new opportunities but persistent discrimination |
| **1920s** | Harlem Renaissance; Garveyism; NAACP growth | Cultural flowering; increased racial consciousness; limited political progress |
| **Great Depression** | Economic collapse; New Deal programs | Devastating poverty; mixed results from federal programs |
| **World War II** | Defense migration; military service; "Double V" | Increased migration; heightened expectations; early civil rights victories |
| **Post-War** | Suburban growth; urban ghettoization; Truman's orders | Residential segregation; legal foundations for later civil rights movement |

This period laid crucial groundwork for the modern civil rights movement, establishing organizations, legal strategies, and heightened expectations that would drive the struggle for equality in the 1950s and 1960s.
