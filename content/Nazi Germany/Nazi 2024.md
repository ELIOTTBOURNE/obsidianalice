
# Assessing the Value of Sources for Studying Nazi Germany's Economic Policies (1933-1945) [30]

## Source Texts

### Source A
**John Gunther, an American journalist, writing in his popular contemporary account of Europe, *Inside Europe* (1938).**  
*The book was written during Hjalmar Schacht’s term of office but published after his resignation in 1937.*  
> "Not only has Schacht been Hitler’s keeper of the purse but he has contrived to make himself indispensable as a link between Hitler and the whole of German economic life. Schacht, beyond any doubt, is one of the ablest financial experts alive. He really understands money. He had the quadruple job of paying for imports, financing public work projects to relieve unemployment, meeting the cost of rearmament and maintaining the ordinary budget of government. Where did Dr Schacht get all the money from?  
> He juggled with internal loans from banks, insurance companies and the like, which were bled almost empty, giving in return government Mefo Bills, worth no more – and no less – than the government’s promise to pay. These loans amounted to nothing less than the compulsory mobilization of the wealth of the German people, and the money has been used to finance a colossal war machine. Externally, Dr Schacht has performed even greater feats of financial wizardry, contriving to pay for imports by getting his creditors to foot the bill, saying ‘I cannot pay my debts unless you lend me more.’ This process reached its most extreme phase back in December 1934, when the Bank of England granted a £750,000 credit to Germany, so that Germany might have means to meet old debts and build aeroplanes that could cross the English Channel in seven minutes."

### Source B
**Adolf Hitler, in a memorandum to his ministers, ordering a Four-Year Plan, which replaced the New Plan (4 September 1936)**  
> "The world has been moving with ever-increasing speed towards a new conflict, which will be with Bolshevism. I therefore draw up the following programme for a final provision of our vital needs:  
> Parallel with the military and political rearmament of our nation must go its economic rearmament and mobilization. In future, the interests of individuals can no longer play any part in these matters. There is only one interest, the interest of the nation; only one view, the bringing of Germany to the point of political and economic self-sufficiency …  
> The job of the Ministry of Economics is simply to set the national economic tasks; private industry has to fulfil them. But if private industry thinks itself incapable of doing this, then the National Socialist State will know how to resolve the problem on its own. Nearly four precious years have now gone by; there has been time enough in four years to find out what we cannot do. Now we have to carry out what we can do.  
> I have set the following tasks:  
> (i) The German armed forces must be operational within four years.  
> (ii) The German economy must be fit for war within four years."

### Source C
**Dr Joseph Goebbels, the Nazi Minister of Propaganda, describes, in his diary, a meeting with the Gauleiters and government ministers (8 October 1943)**  
> "Speer’s industrial experts didn’t say much that was new for me, but their pessimistic descriptions of the state of the war and war production were a good introduction for Speer’s own fifty-minute address. Based on solid facts and figures, he demonstrates as clearly as can be that nothing will now help except total effort for total war, as – for all the good it did – I already said in my speech at the Sportpalast [in February, when I too advocated a vigorous approach to total war].  
> Speer told the Gauleiters very bluntly that no protests and no arguments would deter him from converting all industrial plants to war production. He is, of course, right: the Führer has ordered him to transfer a million workers into armaments at once and, furthermore, to release sufficient young men from the armament industry to form about twenty divisions. The Gauleiters are screaming holy murder [furious], for of course it means the end of most of their flourishing industries. But they’ll have to go along with him."

## Source Analysis

### Source A: John Gunther, *Inside Europe* (1938)
**Context:** Written during Schacht's tenure but published after his 1937 resignation, amid debates about Nazi economic priorities.

**Strengths:**
- Provides contemporary outsider perspective on Schacht's financial innovations
- Details key mechanisms of Nazi economic policy: deficit financing, Mefo Bills, and import strategies
- Highlights the tension between economic recovery and rearmament
- Demonstrates international awareness of Nazi rearmament financing (e.g., Bank of England credit)
- Captures Schacht's significance before his fall from favor

**Limitations:**
- Journalistic account aimed at popular audience lacks technical economic detail
- Gunther's "almost adulatory" tone toward Schacht lacks critical perspective
- Published after Schacht's resignation, potentially missing later developments
- Doesn't explicitly address unemployment reduction or the New Plan
- Limited insight into Nazi ideological motivations behind economic policies

**Value:** High for understanding Schacht's specific financial techniques and international perceptions of Nazi economics in the mid-1930s, but limited for analyzing broader Nazi economic ideology or later developments.

### Source B: Hitler's Four-Year Plan Memorandum (1936)
**Context:** Issued during economic recovery but amid growing tensions over rearmament priorities, replacing Schacht's New Plan.

**Strengths:**
- Direct insight into Hitler's economic thinking and priorities
- Explicitly states the shift toward autarky and war preparation ("guns not butter")
- Reveals the Nazi leadership's approach to state control of the economy
- Shows the integration of economic policy with foreign policy aims
- Demonstrates Hitler's impatience with previous economic approaches
- Primary source evidence of Nazi planning for war economy

**Limitations:**
- Prescriptive document rather than description of actual implementation
- Doesn't address practical challenges of achieving stated goals
- Overstates the regime's ability to control complex economic forces
- No reflection on social consequences of economic policies
- Doesn't acknowledge internal disagreements about economic direction

**Value:** Extremely high for understanding Nazi economic ideology, the shift toward rearmament, and Hitler's personal role in economic planning. Essential for analyzing the regime's economic priorities and the origins of the war economy.

### Source C: Goebbels' Diary Entry (1943)
**Context:** Written after Stalingrad defeat, during the transition to total war economy under Speer's leadership.

**Strengths:**
- Insider perspective on high-level economic decision-making during wartime
- Documents the implementation of total war measures and resistance to them
- Reveals tensions between central economic planners (Speer) and regional leaders (Gauleiters)
- Shows the evolution of Nazi economic policy in response to military setbacks
- Demonstrates Speer's role in rationalizing war production
- Provides evidence of manpower shortages and industrial conversion challenges

**Limitations:**
- Reflects Goebbels' personal biases and propaganda priorities
- Focuses narrowly on armaments production rather than broader economy
- Doesn't address civilian living conditions or resource allocation beyond war needs
- Diary entry captures moment in time rather than comprehensive policy overview
- Limited technical economic detail about production methods or resource allocation

**Value:** High for understanding the late-war Nazi economy, the implementation of total war measures, and internal conflicts over economic management. Particularly valuable for analyzing Speer's role and the practical challenges of maintaining war production.

---

## Collective Value of the Sources

Together, these sources provide a valuable chronological framework for understanding the evolution of Nazi economic policy:

1. **Developmental Timeline:** The sources trace key phases: Schacht's financial management (1933-1937), the Four-Year Plan's autarkic drive (1936-1942), and Speer's total war economy (1942-1945).

2. **Continuity and Change:** Collectively, they show both the continuity of state control over the economy and the changing methods and priorities as Germany moved from recovery to war.

3. **Leadership Perspectives:** The sources offer insights from different viewpoints: an external observer (Gunther), the supreme leader (Hitler), and a key minister (Goebbels), providing multiple angles on economic decision-making.

4. **Key Debates Illuminated:** Together, they help historians address central questions about Nazi economic policy:
   - The balance between ideological goals and practical necessities
   - The effectiveness of state control versus market mechanisms
   - The relationship between economic policy and military requirements
   - The tensions between different Nazi factions over economic direction

**Limitations as a Set:**
- No direct worker or civilian perspectives on economic policies
- Limited technical economic data for quantitative analysis
- Minimal coverage of agricultural policies or consumer goods production
- Scant attention to the role of forced labor in the economy
- No representation of economic policy in occupied territories

---

## Conclusion
These three sources offer substantial collective value to historians studying Nazi economic policies. Source A provides crucial insight into the innovative financial techniques of the early Nazi period, Source B reveals Hitler's ideological vision for economic autarky and rearmament, and Source C documents the implementation of total war measures during the conflict's final phase. While each has limitations regarding perspective and coverage, together they trace the evolution of Nazi economic management from recovery through rearmament to total war. Their greatest value lies in how they complement each other chronologically and thematically, allowing historians to analyze both the continuity of state control and the changing priorities of Nazi economic policy across the entire 1933-1945 period.

## Arguments Supporting the Totalitarian Nature of Nazi Germany

* **Swift Implementation of Dictatorship**: The Enabling Act, ban on other political parties and trade unions, and ruthless elimination of rivals (e.g., the Night of the Long Knives) established dictatorial control. Plebiscites gave the regime appearance of legitimacy, while Hitler's foreign and economic policies gained genuine popularity.

* **Use of Terror**: The police, Schutzstaffel (SS), block wardens, and concentration camps for political opponents not only silenced critics but also deepened and consolidated Nazi control.

* **Propaganda and Cult of Personality**: Control of media and literature, combined with the strength of Hitler's personality cult, facilitated the operation of a one-party state.

* **Youth Indoctrination**: The indoctrination of youth and control of education were carefully calculated to bolster the regime's present and future support.

--- column-break ---
## Arguments Challenging the Totalitarian Nature of Nazi Germany

* **The "Weak Dictator" Debate**: Historians debate whether Hitler was a weak dictator who tolerated overlapping and inefficient state and party structures.

* **Limited Economic Control**: The extent of Nazi control over the economy and business suggests much was outside the party's control. The Nazis often followed public opinion, using polls to shape policy, raising questions about whether the Third Reich was a "consensual dictatorship."

* **Accommodation with Traditional Institutions**: 
  * The regime showed caution toward established Churches by 1939
  * The euthanasia programme was toned down in response to Roman Catholic protests
  * Occasional caution was shown in response to foreign reaction about racial policy before 1938 and during the 1936 Olympic Games

* **Preservation of Certain Freedoms**: Freedom of travel—both internally and externally—is often cited against the notion of a completely totalitarian state.

### Arguments Supporting Hitler's Responsibility

* **Over-reliance on Blitzkrieg**: An inability to gear the economy for total war left Germany vulnerable to a war of attrition against more powerful enemies.

* **Strategic Errors in 1940-1941**: Hitler vacillated over finishing off the United Kingdom, and his reckless decisions to attack the USSR and declare war on the US were major strategic errors that led to Germany's defeat.

* **Military Campaign Failures**: Dismal conduct of the Stalingrad and Tunisian campaigns, for which Hitler must bear primary responsibility, were crucial turning points in the war in 1942–1943.

* **Diplomatic Failures**: Hitler failed to recognise any potential diplomatic solution to Germany's problems, for example a separate peace with the USSR, which some historians believe was possible in 1942–1943 as Stalin was disillusioned with the Western Allies' actions over Italy and what he perceived as foot dragging over the opening of the Second Front.

--- column-break ---
### Arguments Against Hitler's Sole Responsibility

* **Allied Economic Strength**: The overwhelming economic strength of the United Kingdom, the USSR and the US made a German victory insurmountable.

* **Allied Military Assets**: The superior naval and aviation assets of the Allies had begun to tell by 1942–1943; this was particularly evident in the Battle of the Atlantic, as well as the Allies' strategic bombing and flexible amphibious capability. The resilience of the USSR was also increasingly apparent by this time.

* **Improved Allied Military Competence**: This was a major factor by 1942–1943, demonstrated in major campaigns in 1944–1945 on both the Western and Eastern fronts.

* **German Military Leadership Issues**: German military incompetence was not always Hitler's fault:
  * Hitler made the right decisions over the initial attack on France in 1940
  * Goering was ineffective as head of the Luftwaffe
  * Other German military leaders could be held responsible for the folly of over-extension, for example Rommel in North Africa

* **Weak Alliances**: Unreliable allies such as Italy, Rumania and Hungary weakened Germany, as did its ineffective co-ordination with Japan.
