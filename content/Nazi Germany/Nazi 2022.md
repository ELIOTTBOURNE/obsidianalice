

# Assessing the Value of Sources for Studying Nazi Foreign Policy (1933-1941) [30]

## Source A: Temperley's Memorandum (1933)
### EXTRACT:
Brigadier Temperley, chief military adviser to the British delegation at the Geneva Disarmament Conference, in a memorandum to the British Government (May 1933) 

Within weeks of his arrival, Hitler has carried out a revolution and made himself the complete master of Germany. The country has given itself up to a delirium of reawakened nationalism and of the most blatant and dangerous militarism. Can we afford to ignore what is going on behind the scenes in Germany? The intensification of military preparations under the Hitler regime, coupled with the strident appeals to force of the Nazi leaders, not only means a secret German rearmament, but creates an entirely new situation. France, the United States and ourselves should address a stern warning to Germany that there can be no disarmament or equality of status and no relaxation of the Treaty of Versailles unless a complete reversion of present military preparations and tendencies takes place in Germany. Admittedly, this will provoke a crisis and the danger of war will be brought nearer. But Germany knows that she cannot fight at present and we must call her bluff. She is powerless before the French army and our fleet. Hitler for all his bombast must give way. There is a mad dog abroad once more and we must resolutely combine either to ensure its destruction or at least its confinement until the disease has run its course. 

### ANALYSIS
**Context:** Written during Hitler's consolidation of power, as Germany participated in the Geneva Disarmament Talks while secretly planning rearmament.

**Strengths:**
- Provides contemporary insider assessment from a British military advisor directly involved in disarmament negotiations
- Reveals early recognition of Germany's true intentions beneath diplomatic surface
- Documents Germany's strategy of using international forums as camouflage for rearmament
- Demonstrates the fundamental Nazi objective to dismantle the Versailles system
- Shows the gap between German diplomatic rhetoric and actual military ambitions
- Valuable evidence of how Germany's foreign policy tactics were perceived by informed observers

**Limitations:**
- Represents only one delegate's perspective among the British delegation
- Reflects Temperley's personal conviction that a forceful approach against Germany was necessary
- Written from an adversarial British perspective that may overemphasize German military ambitions
- Limited insight into internal Nazi decision-making processes
- Cannot access Hitler's private thoughts or the full scope of Nazi planning

**Value:** High for understanding Nazi Germany's early foreign policy tactics and the regime's approach to undermining Versailles. Particularly valuable for analyzing the gap between Germany's diplomatic posture and actual rearmament plans during 1933-1935. Essential for historians studying the initial phase of Nazi foreign policy when the regime was still establishing its international position.





---

#### Collective Value of the Sources

Together, these sources provide a valuable chronological framework for understanding the evolution of Nazi foreign policy:

1. **Developmental Timeline:** The sources trace key phases: initial diplomatic camouflage (1933-1935), the shift to overt expansionism (1938-1939), and the ideological drive toward eastern expansion (1941).

2. **Continuity and Change:** Collectively, they show both the continuity of Nazi objectives (overturning Versailles, expansion) and the changing methods (diplomatic deception → open aggression → ideological warfare).

3. **Multiple Perspectives:** The sources offer insights from different viewpoints: an external observer (Temperley), the supreme leader (Hitler), and a key foreign policy implementer (Ribbentrop).

4. **Key Debates Illuminated:** Together, they help historians address central questions about Nazi foreign policy:
   - The relationship between ideology and pragmatism in foreign policy decision-making
   - The evolution from revisionism to expansionism
   - The role of propaganda in justifying aggressive foreign policy
   - Hitler's personal role versus institutional influences in shaping foreign policy

**Limitations as a Set:**
- No direct representation of the perspectives of Germany's targets or victims
- Limited insight into internal Nazi debates about foreign policy
- Minimal coverage of economic dimensions of foreign policy
- No sources from the critical period 1939-1940 (Poland to France)
- Scant attention to the role of the military in foreign policy development

---

#### Conclusion
These three sources offer substantial collective value to historians studying Nazi foreign policy from 1933 to 1941. Source A provides crucial insight into the early phase of diplomatic deception and rearmament, Source B reveals the radicalization of Nazi foreign policy during the critical expansionist period of 1938, and Source C documents the ideological justification for the ultimate eastern expansion in 1941. While each source has limitations regarding perspective and reliability, together they trace the evolution of Nazi foreign policy from undermining Versailles through open expansionism to ideological warfare. Their greatest value lies in how they complement each other chronologically and thematically, allowing historians to analyze both the continuity of Nazi objectives and the changing methods of achieving them across this pivotal period. When cross-referenced with other historical evidence, these sources provide essential windows into the development of Nazi foreign policy that ultimately led to World War II.





## Source B: Hitler's Speech (1938)

### EXTRACT:
An extract from a secret speech made by Hitler to representatives of the German press in Munich (November 1938) 
> We have set ourselves several tasks this year which we want to achieve through our propaganda, and I consider the press present here among the main instruments of our propaganda. The first task is the gradual preparation of the German people themselves. For years, circumstances have compelled me to talk about almost nothing but peace. Only by continually stressing Germany’s desire for peace and her peaceful intentions could I achieve freedom for the German people bit by bit and provide armaments which were always necessary before the next step could be taken. It is obvious that such peace propaganda also has its doubtful aspects, for it can only too easily give people the idea that the present regime really identifies itself with the determination to preserve peace at all costs. It is only out of necessity that for years I talked of peace. It is now necessary to re-educate the German people psychologically and to make clear that there are things which must be achieved by force if peaceful means fail. To do this, it will be necessary to depict to the German people certain diplomatic events in such a light that the inner voice of the nation itself gradually begins to call for the use of force. 

**Context:** Delivered after the Anschluss with Austria and during the Sudetenland crisis, as Nazi foreign policy shifted from revisionism to expansionism.

**Strengths:**
- Direct primary source revealing Hitler's thinking and foreign policy intentions
- Demonstrates the evolution from covert to overt expansionist ambitions
- Shows Hitler's communication strategy to cultivate domestic support for aggressive foreign policy
- Reflects growing Nazi confidence after early diplomatic successes
- Reveals the tactical shift from "peaceful" rearmament phase to open expansionism
- Provides evidence of Hitler's ability to create and exploit international opportunities

**Limitations:**
- Speech is propaganda designed for public consumption rather than private policy statement
- May contain rhetorical elements intended to mobilize support rather than reveal precise plans
- Doesn't address internal debates within the Nazi regime about foreign policy direction
- Limited technical detail about specific diplomatic or military strategies
- May overstate Hitler's control over foreign policy developments

**Value:** Extremely high for understanding the radicalization of Nazi foreign policy in 1938-1939. Crucial for analyzing Hitler's personal role in foreign policy, the regime's communication strategies, and the transition from revisionism to expansionism. Essential for historians studying the critical period when Nazi foreign policy moved into a "new gear" of rapid escalation.




## Source C: Ribbentrop's Press Release (1941)
EXTRACT
	Joachim von Ribbentrop, German Foreign Minister, in a press release published in The New York Times (June 1941) 
		> Contrary to all engagements which they have undertaken, the Soviet Government have turned against Germany. They have not only continued but, even since the outbreak of war, intensified subversive activities against Germany and Europe; they have in continually increasing measure, developed their foreign policy in a tendency hostile to Germany; and they have massed their entire forces on the German frontier ready for action. The Soviet Government has violated treaties and broken their agreements with Germany. Bolshevist Moscow’s hatred of National Socialism was stronger than its political wisdom. Bolshevism is opposed to National Socialism in deadly enmity. Bolshevist Moscow is about to stab National Socialist Germany in the back while she is engaged in a struggle for her existence. Germany has no intention of remaining inactive in the face of this grave threat to her eastern frontier. The Fϋhrer has, therefore, ordered German forces to oppose this menace with all the might at their disposal. In the coming struggle the German people are fully aware that they are called upon not only to defend their native land but to save the entire civilized world from the deadly dangers of Bolshevism and clear the way for true social progress in Europe.


**Context:** Issued immediately before Operation Barbarossa, as Germany prepared to invade the Soviet Union despite the Nazi-Soviet Pact.

**Strengths:**
- Primary source documenting the Nazi justification for invading the Soviet Union
- Reveals the propaganda framework used to rationalize Operation Barbarossa
- Demonstrates the role of the Foreign Ministry under Ribbentrop in shaping international perceptions
- Shows the ideological dimension of Nazi foreign policy (anti-Bolshevism)
- Provides evidence of the breakdown of the Nazi-Soviet Pact from the German perspective
- Illustrates how the regime presented military aggression as defensive action

**Limitations:**
- Purely propaganda document designed to mislead international opinion
- Contains false claims about Soviet intentions to attack Germany
- Reflects Ribbentrop's personal skepticism about the wisdom of invading the USSR
- Doesn't reveal the true decision-making process behind Operation Barbarossa
- Lacks insight into Stalin's actual intentions or Soviet perspective
- Presents a distorted version of events to justify German aggression

**Value:** High for understanding Nazi propaganda techniques and the ideological justification for Operation Barbarossa. Particularly valuable for analyzing how the regime framed its eastern expansion as a preventive strike. Essential for historians studying the final phase of Nazi foreign policy before the war's expansion, though it must be cross-referenced with other sources to separate propaganda from reality.
